# Stevin's chain and your laptop

Look at the diagonal on the left. The bottom-left endpoint is a man on a beach in Bruges, in 1586, looking at a chain draped over a wedge and realising &mdash; *with a small jolt of surprise, as anyone who solves a problem worth solving does* &mdash; that the chain has to hang in equilibrium. Simon Stevin sketched the picture in his *Beghinselen der Weeghconst* and wrote, in Dutch, his motto *Wonder en is gheen wonder* &mdash; wonder is no wonder. He had just discovered, without using the word, the parallelogram of forces &mdash; the rule for adding two arrows, the same rule we drew out in the second act of chapter one. The chain had to hang where it did because forces add the way they add. Four hundred and forty years later, you are reading these words on a screen rendered by software that runs on hardware that is, ultimately, a giant cascade of arrows being added together. Stevin drew the first one. You are reading on the last one.

Between the two endpoints is the diagonal you have just walked. Tick marks at every chapter. Newton in the plague year. Gauss, twenty-three years old, predicting where the lost asteroid Ceres would re-emerge in the night sky and being almost exactly right. Legendre and Gauss arguing about who invented least squares, both of them right in the sense that mattered, both of them missing the deeper point &mdash; that least squares is a *projection*, not a formula. Pearson reading Galton's data on parents and children and pulling out, by hand, the first principal axis. Rosenblatt building a hardware perceptron in 1958 and being on the front page of the *New York Times* for it. The 1969 crisis with Minsky and Papert and the thirty-year winter that followed. Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, in 1986, doing on a small computer what nature had been doing in biological brains for half a billion years &mdash; running the chain rule backwards through a stack of layers. LeCun, on the East Coast, reading mail. Mikolov, on the West Coast, embedding words into a space where *king minus man plus woman* lands near *queen*. Bahdanau, in Montreal, inventing attention so that a neural translator could look at the right part of the source sentence when it generated each word of the target. Vaswani et al., in 2017, throwing away the recurrence and keeping only the attention, and accidentally inventing the substrate of every chatbot, every coding assistant, every research agent, every system the next five years would put in front of you.

There is a habit in popular writing about AI of presenting this lineage as a sequence of *breakthroughs* &mdash; isolated moments of genius, each one rendering the previous ones obsolete. The truth is rougher and, I think, more interesting. Most of the diagonal is *increments*. Stevin's chain is the *increment* of an idea that Aristotle and Archimedes had circled without quite touching. Newton's fluxions are the *increment* of an idea that Eudoxus and Cavalieri had set up centuries earlier. Gauss's least squares is the *increment* of an idea Legendre had published four years before and that everyone agreed in retrospect Roger Cotes had foreshadowed in 1722. Backpropagation is the *increment* of Werbos in 1974, who got it from Linnainmaa in 1970, who got it from automatic differentiation papers in the 1960s. ReAct, the paper that opened this chapter, is the *increment* of chain-of-thought prompting, which was itself the increment of self-consistent decoding, which was itself the increment of in-context learning, which was itself an unexpected emergent capability of large transformers nobody had specifically tried to build. The diagonal you see is one continuous line because *every point along it is one small step from the previous point.* The line looks steep only because there are so many small steps and they keep happening.

I think that is a useful frame for what to do next. The frame is not *you must invent a breakthrough*. The frame is: *you can take a small step from where you are.* The diagonal admits many people. There is room for a step that is your step.

So &mdash; what next.

*Build something*. The smallest thing that has all the parts: a model, a tool, a loop. A weather agent. A small coding helper. A research assistant for a domain you care about. Wire one tool. Watch it fail. Reflect, in writing. Try again. The hour you spend doing this will teach you more than the hour you spend reading the next paper.

*Read* Mathematics for Machine Learning, Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, freely available online at mml-book.com. It should feel readable now. It wouldn't have, before chapter one. That difference is the entire purpose of the book you just finished.

*Read* Sutton and Barto's *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*, second edition, also free online. We touched RL only obliquely &mdash; in RLHF, in chapter 24 &mdash; and the field is much richer than the small slice we got. Sutton and Barto is a friendly book; it is also the book the entire RL community still cites.

*Watch* the safety community. Anthropic's *Responsible Scaling Policy* posts. OpenAI's preparedness framework. DeepMind's safety org. METR, ARC, the Long-Term Resilience Institute, the academic safety groups at MIT, Berkeley, Oxford, NYU, and the long unglamorous list of independent researchers thinking carefully about what *should* happen even when nobody else is watching. This is, by an order of magnitude, the most important work happening in AI today, and it gets less attention than it deserves because the deliverables are *absences of bad outcomes*, which are hard to point at.

A book ends. The field doesn't. The diagonal goes one more tick to the right tomorrow morning, and then one more the morning after. You have walked the previous four hundred and forty years of it with me. The next tick, with luck and with care, is yours.

Thank you for reading.
