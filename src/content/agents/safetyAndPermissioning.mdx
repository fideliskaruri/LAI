# How much trust, how slowly, on what evidence

Drag the agent up and down the ladder on the left. There are four rungs. At the bottom &mdash; *read-only* &mdash; the agent can look at the world but not change it. One rung up &mdash; *sandbox write* &mdash; it can write to a scratch folder, hit mock APIs, do whatever it wants inside a contained playground. Two rungs up &mdash; *real action with approval* &mdash; it can touch the real world (your calendar, your repo, your bank), but every action waits for a human *yes*. At the top &mdash; *autonomous* &mdash; it does long-horizon work with no human in the per-step loop. The right pane shows the risk profile and the guardrail at each rung. Drag the agent up and you watch the risk grow, the guardrails change shape, and the consent story get harder.

A claim I want to make explicitly, because it is the moral spine of this chapter and easy to skim past. *Capability and consent are independent gradients, and the field is currently miscalibrated on both.* We have got very good at making the capability gradient rise &mdash; the agent of 2026 can do, with a casual prompt, things the agent of 2022 couldn't do at all. We have *not* got good at making the consent gradient rise at the same rate. The consent gradient is the question: how does the user know what the agent will do before it does it; how does the user authorise it; how can the user undo it; how can a regulator, a court, or a coroner reconstruct what happened. The thesis of this act is that *those two gradients have to move together*, and that they currently don't.

The four rungs are a simplification &mdash; in practice the ladder is continuous &mdash; but they map cleanly to four kinds of system in deployment today.

*Read-only.* The simplest case. The agent is a research assistant, a summariser, a question-answerer with web access. It produces a chunk of text. You read it. You act on it or you don't. The blast radius of a wrong answer is the time you waste reading it. The guardrail is *you re-read it before acting on it*. This is how almost every consumer agent system shipped in 2023 worked, and it is still the safest design pattern available. It has one big virtue: the user is the executor. The agent is the advisor. Disagreement is cheap because nothing has happened yet.

*Sandbox write.* The agent can change things, but only in a contained playground. A code-execution sandbox without network access. A scratch directory it can write to. A staging database that mirrors production. The agent does its work; the changes accumulate in the sandbox; at the end, a human reviews and either promotes the work to the real world or throws it away. The blast radius is *the sandbox*. The guardrail is *the snapshot-and-revert*. Almost every modern coding agent works this way: it edits files inside a worktree or a container; the developer sees a diff; the diff merges or doesn't. This rung trades latency (someone has to review) for safety (no irreversible action without review). The trade is usually worth it.

*Real action with approval.* Now the agent touches the live world but each touch is gated. The agent says *I want to send this calendar invite to this person*; the user clicks *approve*; the invite goes. The agent says *I want to spend $300 on this flight*; the user clicks *approve*; the card is charged. This is the rung consumer agents are mostly settling on today &mdash; the per-action approval is what makes the model's mistakes containable. The risk is no longer *the model is wrong* (the model is always sometimes wrong); the risk is *the user clicks approve without reading*. This is a real failure mode, well studied in the privacy literature. People are bad at reading approval dialogs after the third or fourth one. The guardrail at this rung is therefore *not just the dialog* but the *visual diff before the commit* &mdash; show me what's about to change, in a form I can scan in two seconds, not in a wall of text I'll skip.

*Autonomous.* The frontier. The agent runs for hours or days on a long-horizon task with no per-step human in the loop. There is, by design, no per-action approval. The user, having authorised the task at the start, lets the agent finish. This is how AutoGPT in 2023 advertised itself, how Devin from Cognition advertised itself in 2024, and how the most ambitious production agents in 2026 work. The risks at this rung are *qualitatively* different from the lower rungs. *Compounding errors* &mdash; a small mistake on step 3 propagates and gets worse by step 30. *Intent drift* &mdash; the agent, sufficiently far down a long task, starts pursuing the surface goal in ways the user wouldn't have endorsed. *Adversarial inputs* &mdash; a page the agent reads in step 17 has a prompt injection that subtly redirects its remaining steps. The guardrails at this rung are *policy* (the agent has rules about what it can never do, regardless of instructions), *kill-switches* (the user can stop it at any moment), *audit trails* (every action is logged, reviewable, attributable). These are the open problems of the field. They are not solved. They are being worked on, hard, by Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy team, by OpenAI's preparedness framework, by DeepMind's safety org, by academic labs at MIT, Berkeley, Oxford, NYU, and the long list of independent research groups in alignment. If you find yourself working on agents at any of these labs &mdash; or, more likely for the reader of this book, at the engineering teams that *deploy* agents into real products &mdash; this is the work that matters most.

I want to close with a small honest observation about my own position. I work at Microsoft. I work on agents that help Microsoft employees navigate compliance obligations. I have, professionally, spent the last two years thinking about exactly the question this act is about. I have not, professionally, resolved it. What I have done is develop a sharp respect for two things at once: *how capable* a well-prompted modern agent is when given good tools, and *how unprepared* most users (myself included) are for the consent dialogs that ought to gate those capabilities. The ladder, in 2026, is real. The agent is climbing it. The reader of this book is, at the very least, equipped to ask the questions that come with each rung. That is a small amount of progress. Most progress, by a long way, is small.

One act to go &mdash; the closing diagonal, from a chain on a wedge in 1586 to the present.
