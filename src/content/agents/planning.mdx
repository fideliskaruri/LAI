# The plan is the tree

Drag the depth slider on the left. At depth zero you see only the root goal: *plan a three-day Rome trip for two under €1000*. Slide it up to depth one and the root explodes into five subgoals &mdash; flights, hotel, food, activities, transport. Slide it to depth two and each subgoal explodes again into the concrete leaf actions an agent would actually execute. Tap any node on the tree and the right pane shows that node in close-up: its name, what it breaks into if it's an interior node, or the actual tool call it would emit if it's a leaf.

A plan, at root, is a *recursive decomposition of a goal into actions*. Nothing more, nothing less. The classical AI literature on planning &mdash; STRIPS in 1971, hierarchical task networks in the 80s, the whole tradition that culminated in the planning competition that ran every year from 1998 to about 2018 &mdash; was preoccupied with how to find such a tree given a description of a world and a goal in it. The algorithms were famously hard. They were also, famously, fragile. A planner that assumed the world matched its symbolic description would shatter the first time the world disagreed. (One famous example, due to John McCarthy: *plan how to get from your house to the airport*; the classical planner says *take car to airport*; the world says *the car has a flat tyre*; the planner has nothing to say about flat tyres because flat tyres weren't in its world model.) Classical planners failed not because they couldn't plan but because they couldn't *replan*, and the world is fundamentally a replanning problem.

Language-model agents finesse this by being mediocre planners that replan constantly. Instead of finding the optimal tree at the start, an LM agent sketches a tree, executes the leftmost leaf, looks at what came back, and revises the rest of the tree in light of the new evidence. The tree is *fluid*. The agent doesn't commit to it; it uses it as a working hypothesis, a way of organising thought. If the cheapest London-to-Rome flight is sold out, the agent doesn't crash; it rewrites that subtree. If the hotel it picked has no availability, the agent rewrites that subtree too. The robustness comes from the fact that the agent is, at every step, *willing to throw out the plan it just wrote*. Classical planners couldn't bring themselves to do this. Their plans were brittle because they were *committed*.

The decomposition you see on the canvas was hand-written for the example. In practice it would be generated by the model itself, in response to a prompt like:

*"Break this goal into 3 to 7 immediate subgoals. For each subgoal, list the tools you'd need."*

The model writes the breakdown in English; the runtime parses it; the agent then iterates over the subgoals, executing each one with a smaller ReAct loop. Recursion is possible: a subgoal can itself be passed back to the model for further breakdown. This is, broadly, how AutoGPT in early 2023 worked, and how every long-horizon agent since works. You give the agent a goal. The agent makes a plan. The agent executes the plan. The agent revises the plan. The agent stops when it thinks it's done.

Two practical observations.

*One.* The tree is almost never balanced. Some subgoals fan out into many leaves; others are leaves themselves. The Rome example has *flights* fanning into a search-and-hold pair, and *transport* collapsing into a single action (*buy the Roma Pass*). This asymmetry is normal &mdash; in fact it's a sign the model is paying attention. A plan that decomposes every subgoal into the same number of sub-subgoals is a plan generated by a process that doesn't understand the goal.

*Two.* The leaves *must* be concrete tool calls. This is the rule that turns a wish into a plan. If the leaves are still goals (*"find a hotel"* without a specific tool call), the plan isn't ready to execute &mdash; the agent will have to decompose further. The act of planning is, mechanically, *the act of pushing the leaves down until they are tool calls.* Tap one of the leaf dots on the canvas and look at the right pane &mdash; the leaf is `web_search({ query: "Rome hotel Monti €120 review" })` or `browser({ url: "booking.com", action: "reserve" })`. Concrete. Executable. Tied to a real tool from the previous act.

There is also a deeper question lurking under the planning surface: *where does the tree come from in the first place?* The answer, again, is that the language model writes it. The model has read, in its training data, a great many examples of human plans &mdash; how-to articles, travel guides, project breakdowns, recipes, software design documents. Those have left a prior in the model about how goals tend to decompose. When you ask the model to break down *plan a Rome trip*, you are sampling from that prior; the model is, in a sense, recalling what humans have, on average, considered a sensible breakdown of similar goals. This is why agent plans look reasonable on the *first* try &mdash; they encode an enormous amount of practical human know-how that the model picked up by reading the open web.

It's also why they are, occasionally, exactly *wrong* on the first try. The prior is good but it isn't infallible. The model doesn't know that your travel partner is vegetarian, that you've already been to Rome twice, that the budget is in yen and not euros. The planning loop has to be open to correction. *Replan*, again, is the verb that matters.

Next act: memory. A plan that took five minutes to write needs to *survive* until the agent gets back to it after a tool call.
