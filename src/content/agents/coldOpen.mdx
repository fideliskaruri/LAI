# A transformer learned to act

On a Thursday in October 2022, six authors at Princeton and one at Google posted a thirty-three-page preprint to arXiv. The paper was numbered 2210.03629. Its title was *ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models*, and the seven names on it were Shunyu Yao, Jeffrey Zhao, Dian Yu, Nan Du, Izhak Shafran, Karthik Narasimhan, and Yuan Cao. It was not, on the surface, a paper about *agents*. It was a paper about prompting. The authors had noticed that if you asked a language model to alternate, in writing, between thinking out loud and taking small actions, it got dramatically better at multi-step tasks &mdash; question answering with web access, navigating a simulated household, sorting through a Wikipedia article in search of a fact. The trick was almost embarrassingly simple. Tag each line of the model's output as either a **thought**, an **action**, or, after the runtime had executed the action, an **observation**. Let the model write the next line of its own choosing. Repeat until the model emitted an answer.

That paper, and the loop it crystallised, is the spine of this chapter.

You have, by this point in the book, met the language model in chapter 23 &mdash; a transformer pretrained on a few trillion tokens of text, fine-tuned on instructions and feedback to behave conversationally. It is, fundamentally, a *next-token predictor*. Given a prompt, it samples a likely next word; given the prompt plus that word, it samples another. Run it long enough and it produces fluent English. Run it inside a chat interface and you have a chatbot. None of what we have done with it so far has involved the model *doing* anything in the world. It speaks; we read; we reply; it speaks again. Its hands are tied behind its back.

ReAct untied the hands. Or, more precisely, ReAct named a pattern in which a person could untie the hands gradually, one tool at a time, while keeping the loop tight enough that humans could still read what the model was thinking before each action. The pattern was: *think, act, observe*. The model emits a sentence of reasoning in plain English (`THOUGHT: I need today's weather in Berlin`). It emits a function call in a structured format (`ACTION: web_search({ query: "weather Berlin today" })`). The runtime parses the call, runs the tool, and feeds the result back to the model (`OBSERVATION: Berlin · cloudy · 14 °C`). The model writes the next thought. The loop continues. At some point the model decides it has what it needs and emits `ANSWER:` instead of `ACTION:`, and the loop stops. That's it. That is the entirety of what a "language-model agent" is, technically. Everything else &mdash; the tool use, the planning, the memory, the reflection, the safety story &mdash; is a refinement of that one loop.

There are now whole companies built on this loop, and there will be more by the time you finish this paragraph. The lineage from ReAct in October 2022 to where we are now is, by the standards of any other technical field, vertical. *Toolformer*, published by a Meta team in February 2023, showed that the model could *teach itself* which tools to call by inserting tool-call training examples into its pretraining mix; the model didn't even need to be prompted to use tools, it could learn the affordance the way it learned anything else. *Reflexion*, by Noah Shinn and colleagues at Northeastern in March 2023, added an outer loop: after the agent finished a task, it would *reflect in writing* on what it had done wrong, and that reflection would go into the prompt for its next attempt &mdash; learning without touching the weights at all. *AutoGPT*, an open-source project released by Toran Bruce Richards under the alias Significant Gravitas on the 30th of March 2023, took the loop, removed the human from each step, and let the model run for hours or days at a time on long-horizon tasks. A few months later it was the fastest-growing software project on GitHub. In October 2024 Anthropic announced *Computer Use*, a model trained explicitly to interact with a screen and a mouse the way a person would, taking screenshots and clicking on pixels. By the time you read this sentence, the descendants of ReAct are booking your flights, writing your code, doing your research, and arguing with each other about it.

The aesthetic of this final chapter is going to be a little different from the rest of the book. We are not building up to a single mathematical idea. The math, oddly, is mostly behind us &mdash; the agent is a language model is a transformer is attention is a stack of linear maps and softmaxes. What is *new* in this chapter is not math but *architecture* at a higher level: how to wire a model that thinks to a world that has tools and people in it, how to give it memory across the gap between turns, how to let it plan, how to let it reflect, and &mdash; the part that none of us has solved yet &mdash; how to do all of this responsibly while the gradient of capability runs steeply ahead of the gradient of consent.

The acts on the canvas are, in order: the loop itself; tools as hands; planning as a tree of subgoals; memory in three flavours; reflection as verbal reinforcement; the permission ladder; and finally a closing diagonal from a man on a beach in 1586 to a piece of software that does your taxes in 2026. There are seven acts after this one. They are the last interactive surface in the book.
