# Forty years of seeing

The left pane is a lineage: four nodes, four years, one idea. It begins in **1980** with **Kunihiko Fukushima** in Tokyo. He published a paper in *Biological Cybernetics* titled *Neocognitron: A Self-organizing Neural Network Model for a Mechanism of Pattern Recognition Unaffected by Shift in Position*. The architecture had alternating "*simple cells*" and "*complex cells*", named after the cells of the same name in **David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel's** 1959 visual-cortex experiments on cats. Fukushima's simple cells detected local features by sliding small templates across the input; his complex cells aggregated nearby simple-cell responses to gain a small amount of position invariance. He had, structurally, the *convolution-then-pool* architecture nine years before LeCun. What he didn't have was a learning algorithm for it. The weights were either hand-designed or set by an unsupervised competitive rule. His digit recognition worked, but barely.

In **1989** LeCun published the paper we opened the chapter with. The architecture was Fukushima's, give or take; the training method was the *backpropagation* algorithm we built in the previous chapter. The network had three layers, was trained on five thousand handwritten ZIP-code digits, and ran in production reading mail by 1991. In 1998 he and Léon Bottou and Yoshua Bengio and Patrick Haffner extended it into the seven-layer **LeNet-5** drawn in the previous act, trained it on MNIST, and shipped it inside an AT&T silicon chip called **ANNA** that read tens of millions of bank cheques each month for nearly a decade.

The field then went quiet. Through the 1990s and 2000s, mainstream computer vision converged on **hand-engineered features** — SIFT (Lowe, 1999), HOG (Dalal and Triggs, 2005), Haar cascades (Viola and Jones, 2001) — fed into classifiers like SVMs (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995). The dominant intuition was that learned features were a curiosity. Then, in **September 2012**, **Alex Krizhevsky**, a student of Geoffrey Hinton at the University of Toronto, ran a deeper version of LeCun's idea — eight layers, ReLU activations, two consumer GPUs, sixty million parameters, dropout, ImageNet's 1.2 million high-resolution photographs — and won the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge by *eleven percentage points*. The runner-up used hand-engineered features. **AlexNet** ended the debate so completely that within a year every serious computer-vision lab had converted. The convolution had won.

For the next eight years, convolutional networks dominated. They became the recommendation engine of YouTube and Pinterest, the perception layer of every self-driving car, the diagnostic backbone of medical imaging, the technical basis of nearly every photograph that touched a phone after 2014. And then in 2020 a team at Google Research published **An Image is Worth 16x16 Words** — the **Vision Transformer**. The convolution was no longer the only way. The architecture of the *next* chapter, *attention*, turned out to work just as well, and at sufficient scale, *better*, on images. We are now in the long quiet handoff. Modern systems mix both.

But the idea has not aged. *Share weights across space.* It was true in Fukushima's Tokyo, true in LeCun's Holmdel, true in Krizhevsky's Toronto, and it remains true today wherever a neural network looks at a picture. **The right pane is a thread card forward to the next chapter.** Convolutions are *local* — every weight sees a small neighbourhood, and the same weight is reused at every neighbourhood across the image. Attention is *global* — each part of the input can look at any other part, with no spatial inductive bias at all. Convolutions hard-wire the assumption that *nearby things matter*. Attention drops that assumption and lets the network learn what to look at, from scratch, on whatever data we feed it.

That is a stronger bet. It is also a more expensive one. Whether it was the right bet depends on what you have a lot of. The next chapter is about the architecture that made the bet, and about the data scale at which the bet started to pay off.
