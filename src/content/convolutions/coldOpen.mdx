# The neural net that read your mail

By 1995, on a quiet day in Holmdel, New Jersey, a custom co-processor sitting on a server rack was reading roughly one of every ten letters in the United States mail. Each envelope passing under a camera was photographed, the ZIP-code window was cropped out by an older piece of optical character recognition, and the five handwritten digits were handed, one at a time, to a small neural network. The network had been trained on tens of thousands of postal examples and a now-famous research dataset called **MNIST**. It guessed each digit, the system reassembled the ZIP, and a downstream sorter pushed the letter into the right bin. Within a few years the same architecture, embedded in a chip called *ANNA*, was reading the courtesy amounts on roughly 15% of all paper bank cheques in the United States. Computer vision, as a *deployed industrial technology*, dates to that moment. It is six years younger than this book's reader, perhaps; perhaps a little older.

The paper that started it ran in the inaugural volume of a new journal called *Neural Computation* in the winter of 1989. The title was *Backpropagation Applied to Handwritten Zip Code Recognition*. The first author was a 29-year-old French postdoc named **Yann LeCun**. The six other co-authors &mdash; Bernhard Boser, John Denker, Donnie Henderson, Richard Howard, Wayne Hubbard, and Larry Jackel &mdash; were colleagues in the Adaptive Systems Research Department of **AT&T Bell Laboratories**, the same Bell Labs that had given the world the transistor, the laser, the C programming language, and Unix. Backpropagation, the trick we just spent a chapter on, had been published in *Nature* three years earlier. What LeCun and his colleagues did was *specialise* it for images.

Their key insight was simple, and on inspection feels obvious. The MLP of the previous chapter is a beautiful thing, but it has a fatal weakness for pictures. An MLP treats every input pixel as if it were unrelated to every other pixel &mdash; a flat list of numbers, in arbitrary order. Take an MNIST digit at 28×28 and feed it to an MLP and the first hidden layer needs *28 × 28 = 784* independent weights per neuron. That's expensive, but worse than expensive: it's *wrong*. The pixels of an image aren't a flat list; they're a grid. Pixels nearby in space are part of the same stroke, the same edge, the same loop. And the *kind* of pattern that distinguishes a 3 from an 8 &mdash; an open loop on the left, a curl on the right &mdash; is the same kind of pattern wherever it appears in the image. If you can detect a loop in the upper-right corner, you should be able to detect the same loop in the lower-left corner, with the same weights, without having to re-learn it.

The conclusion is then forced. Don't let the hidden neurons have arbitrary weights over arbitrary subsets of the input. *Constrain* them. Make each hidden neuron look only at a small local patch of the image &mdash; a 5×5 neighborhood, say. And make every hidden neuron at the same depth use the *same* small bank of weights, sliding it across the image. This single constraint, when you wire it up and train it with backpropagation, turns the network from a generic pattern matcher into a *vision system*. The constraint has a name. It's called a **convolution**.

The convolution had been used in image processing for decades before 1989 &mdash; for edge detection, smoothing, sharpening &mdash; with hand-designed weights chosen by humans. The reason LeNet was different is that the convolution's weights were not handed down by a human. They were *learned*, end to end, by the same backpropagation algorithm we built last chapter. The 1989 paper threw a small convolutional network at handwritten digits and got 5% error. Within a decade, on the same task, the descendants of that network were below 1%. They were trained on a few million examples, they ran on hardware that fit on a graduate student's desk, and they read mail.

Then the field stalled. Throughout the 1990s and 2000s the prevailing intuition in mainstream computer vision was that hand-engineered features &mdash; SIFT, HOG, Haar cascades, all designed by clever people who understood image geometry &mdash; would always beat learned ones. Neural networks were considered a curiosity. Until **AlexNet** arrived at the 2012 ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge, ran the same basic idea on a million high-resolution photographs at a scale six orders of magnitude bigger than LeNet, won by an unprecedented eleven percentage points, and ended the debate forever. Convolutional networks then dominated computer vision for the next decade, until a competing idea called *attention* &mdash; the topic of the next chapter &mdash; began to catch up.

The chapter is the technical content of LeCun's 1989 idea. We will walk through what an image *is* to a computer. We will build a small kernel and slide it across the picture. We will watch a feature map fill in, cell by cell. We will toggle between four different kernels and notice that each one tells a different story about the image. We will introduce a deliberate kind of forgetting called *pooling* that shrinks the data and buys position invariance. We will stack two convolution-and-pool blocks and notice that the deeper layers do something philosophically different from the shallow ones. And we will close with a thread forward to the architectures of the present.

The previous chapter was about how the *weights* learn. This one is about how the *wiring* of the network can encode a prior about the world &mdash; that nearby things matter and that the same pattern can appear anywhere. *Wiring as prior knowledge.* That is the move, and convolutions are the first place we meet it.
