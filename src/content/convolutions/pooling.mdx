# Throwing away three-quarters of the data, on purpose

Drag the vermilion window on the left. It is a *2×2 window* sliding across the feature map produced by the vertical-edge kernel from the previous act. Wherever the window lands, it covers four cells. The bottom strip of the left pane shows those four values and their *maximum*. That maximum is the *one cell* of the right pane that the window's position drives. As you drag the window across the input, the corresponding cell of the output grid lights up &mdash; and the output grid is exactly half as wide and half as tall as the input. Twenty-five cells instead of one hundred.

This operation has a name. It's called **2×2 max-pooling with stride 2**, and the *2×2* is the window size, the *stride 2* is the step (the window jumps by two cells at a time, so adjacent windows don't overlap), and *max* is the rule for combining four cells into one. (You could also average them; that's called *average pooling*. The max version is far more common in modern CNNs because the strongest activation in a small neighbourhood is the most informative summary &mdash; "*there was an edge somewhere in this 2×2 patch*", which is usually what you care about.) The arithmetic of one pooled cell is the simplest thing in the chapter: *pick the largest of four numbers and write it down.*

So why do this? It looks like vandalism &mdash; we just spent two acts carefully extracting a feature map from the image, and now we are deliberately throwing away three of every four cells. There are three reasons, and each of them is good.

**One: shrinkage.** Pooling reduces the spatial size of the feature map by half in each direction, so the number of cells drops by a factor of four. A 28×28 feature map becomes a 14×14 feature map. Then a 7×7. Then a 4×4. Then a 2×2. The size is shrinking *exponentially* as you go deeper, which is exactly what you need: the early layers of the network have *a lot* of cells (high spatial resolution) and the deep layers have *few* cells (low spatial resolution, but rich semantic content). Without pooling, every layer of a network would have the same spatial extent as the input image, which is enormously expensive. The 1989 LeCun network ran on a single CPU; the network's compute budget literally could not afford a constant-spatial-extent stack of convolutions. Pooling makes deep networks possible.

**Two: position invariance.** Suppose you take the original digit *3* and shift it one pixel to the right. The pre-pooling feature map looks subtly different: every cell of the original feature map now sits one column to the right, and where there used to be a bright cell there's now a less-bright cell. But the *2×2 max* of any patch is the *maximum* &mdash; the brightest of four. If the brightest cell shifts by one position within its 2×2 box, the max stays the same. The pooled output is invariant to small shifts. *The digit can move by a pixel; the answer survives.* That tolerance, summed across many pooling layers, is what allowed LeCun's network to read handwritten digits which the writers had centred sloppily, scaled differently, written at slight angles &mdash; the network was robust to *small* deformations because every pooling layer kept the answer steady under a tiny shift.

**Three: enlarged receptive fields.** This one is subtle but important. A 3×3 kernel directly above a 12×12 image can only "see" a 3×3 patch &mdash; a *receptive field* of nine pixels. But a 3×3 kernel applied to a *pooled* feature map can see a 3×3 region of the pooled grid, which corresponds to a *6×6 region of the original image* (each pooled cell is the max of four input cells, and adjacent pooled cells come from adjacent 2×2 patches). So pooling *expands the effective receptive field* without growing the kernel itself. Stack a few pool-and-conv blocks together and a small kernel deep in the network is looking at an enormous fraction of the original image. By layer five of LeNet, a 5×5 kernel has a receptive field of roughly 30×30 input pixels, which is the entire MNIST digit.

These three reasons explain why pooling, despite being lossy, is *useful* lossy. It throws away spatial detail to gain shrinkage, robustness, and reach. The lost information &mdash; "*where exactly was the brightest cell within that 2×2 patch?*" &mdash; is exactly the information we don't care about for classification. *Is there a vertical edge somewhere in this 2×2 patch?* matters. *Was it in the upper-left or the lower-right cell?* does not.

A small modern note. The history of pooling has been a long quiet argument. Throughout the 2000s and early 2010s, 2×2 max-pool was the dominant choice. In the late 2010s, the field discovered that *strided convolution* &mdash; a convolution that skips every other input cell &mdash; could do the same shrinking job, while also being *learnable* (the kernel weights are trained; the pooling step has no learnable parameters). Many modern architectures, including ResNet's later variants and Vision Transformers' patch embeddings, use strided convolutions instead of explicit pooling. The role is the same; only the wiring changes. Pooling remains the conceptual ancestor and the easiest version to draw.

We have kernels. We have feature maps. We have pooling. We have, in fact, the entire vocabulary of LeNet. The next act stacks them into a real architecture and asks what happens when you do this *twice*.
