# Four kernels, four pictures of the same digit

Tap any of the four chips below the image. The kernel in the upper-left of the left pane changes; the feature map on the right repaints. The image itself doesn't change at all. What you're watching is the *same picture*, interpreted through *four different filters*, each filter answering its own question about the picture and visualising the answer as a 10×10 grid of "yes I see this here" / "no I don't" responses.

The first chip is the Sobel vertical-edge kernel we just spent two acts on. The feature map lights up along the *vertical* contrasts in the digit &mdash; the down-stroke of the *3*. The second chip is its horizontal cousin, with the negative weights on the top row and positive weights on the bottom. The feature map for this one lights up along the *horizontal* contrasts in the digit &mdash; the tops of the loops, where bright paper turns to dark ink and back. The vertical-edge kernel barely notices these horizontal contrasts; the horizontal-edge kernel barely notices the vertical ones. *Two questions about the same picture; two complementary answers.*

The third chip is a **box blur**. All nine kernel weights are *1/9*. Multiply each of the nine patch intensities by *1/9* and sum: that's the *average intensity* of the patch. The feature map of a blur kernel is *the image, smoothed*. Every cell holds the local mean. Sharp features get blurred together; ink and paper blend at the boundary. This is what you do to noisy images to clean them, and what you do to high-resolution images to downsample them. It's not exciting visually, but it's important historically &mdash; the *averaging operator* is one of the oldest things in image processing, and you can see, in the regular structure of the blur kernel, where the idea comes from.

The fourth chip is a **sharpening** kernel. Centre weight *+5*; up/down/left/right weights *−1*; corner weights *0*. The convolution becomes *five times the centre pixel minus the sum of its four neighbours*, which mathematically is the discrete approximation to *(centre) − (Laplacian of centre)*, which in practical terms accentuates anywhere the centre pixel is brighter than its neighbours, and darkens anywhere it's dimmer. The feature map sharpens the edges; the digit appears crisper. This is the kernel many photo editors call "Unsharp Mask," and it lived on every Macintosh in 1990 as a one-click filter.

The conceptual upshot is the same in each case. *The image is the same; the question is different.* Each kernel encodes a particular question about local structure &mdash; "*are there vertical edges here?*", "*are there horizontal edges here?*", "*what is the local average?*", "*how much does this pixel stand out from its neighbours?*" &mdash; and the feature map is the answer at every location. If you want to know *both* whether there are vertical edges *and* whether there are horizontal edges *and* whether the local average is high, you simply run *all three* kernels in parallel on the same image and stack their feature maps as three *channels* of a single output. That stacking is exactly what a real convolutional layer does. A LeNet-style first convolutional layer has six kernels and produces six feature maps; an AlexNet first layer has 96 kernels and produces 96 feature maps; a modern ResNet has 64 to 256 kernels per early layer. *More kernels = more questions about the image asked in parallel.*

But here is the punchline of the chapter, and the conceptual centre of computer vision. *Nobody hand-designs these kernels in a real CNN.* The four shown here are pretty &mdash; their numbers were chosen by Irwin Sobel and by photo-editing engineers and by Newton in his theory of the Laplacian. They are pleasant little pieces of human craftsmanship. In a real convolutional neural network, the kernel weights are initialised *randomly* &mdash; nine numbers drawn from a Gaussian &mdash; and then *trained*, by exactly the backpropagation algorithm we built last chapter, to minimise the classification loss. After training on millions of labelled images, the kernels in the first convolutional layer *come to look like* Sobel-style edge detectors, oriented at every angle, plus blob detectors and colour-blob detectors and local-contrast detectors. Nobody hand-designs them; they *emerge*. Krizhevsky's AlexNet 2012 paper has a famous figure showing the 96 first-layer kernels its training discovered. They look, to the unprepared eye, suspiciously like the catalogue of filters that decades of image-processing researchers had designed by hand.

This is the deep claim of representation learning, brought into vision. *The relevant features of an image &mdash; the features that distinguish 3s from 8s, cats from dogs, ships from planes &mdash; can be discovered, automatically, by gradient descent on the task.* You don't have to know what to detect. You don't have to design the detectors. The data tells the network what's worth detecting, and the network puts it into its kernels. The shape of the wiring &mdash; the convolution itself &mdash; supplies the prior that *whatever is worth detecting is detected with the same weights at every location*. The weights themselves are filled in by the loss.

You can think of the four kernels in this act as a tiny, hand-designed first layer of a vision system from 1968. The next act introduces another idea from that era &mdash; *pooling* &mdash; which is what lets the network shrink and become tolerant to small shifts. With kernels and pooling together, we have all the parts we need to stack a real CNN.
