# The workhorse

What we've built in this chapter is, by some distance, the most important algorithm in modern AI. *Gradient descent.* Compute the gradient. Step against it. Repeat.

The next chapter, *Logistic regression*, will give us a model whose loss has no closed form. We'll train it by gradient descent.

The chapter after that, the *Perceptron*, was historically trained by a special-case algorithm of Rosenblatt's, but is best understood now as a particular variant of gradient descent on a hinge-shaped loss. Same algorithm.

*MLP / backpropagation* (chapter 18) is gradient descent through a stack of layers, with a clever trick — the chain rule we built in Derivatives, applied recursively — for computing the gradient efficiently. Same algorithm.

*Convolutional networks*, *Transformers*, *language models*, *RLHF*, *diffusion* — all of them trained by some variant of stochastic gradient descent. The variants matter for practical performance (Adam, RMSprop, Adagrad, Lion, momentum, weight decay, learning-rate schedules) but the *underlying move* is the one Cauchy wrote down in 1847. Compute the gradient. Step against it. Repeat.

*Support vector machines* (chapter 17), *Gaussian mixtures* (16, via EM), and even some classical statistical methods are best understood as gradient-style algorithms on appropriately chosen losses.

The exception, in the chapters left in this platform, is anywhere a closed form exists — and that exception is shrinking. We saw it for linear regression. Principal Component Analysis (next-but-one, chapter 14) has a closed form in terms of the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix. K-means (chapter 15) has an iterative algorithm that isn't gradient descent but is structurally similar in spirit. Everything past that, gradient descent.

(I find it hard to overstate how much of modern machine learning is just *this*. There's a tendency, when learning about a new neural network architecture, to think that the architecture is the interesting part. It usually isn't. The interesting part — the part that actually distinguishes a model that works from one that doesn't — is the *loss function*, the *data*, and the *training trajectory*. The architecture chooses the *shape* of the loss surface; the data populates it with real values; the training trajectory follows the gradient down to a good minimum. The architecture is a parameterization, not a procedure. The procedure is always gradient descent.)

A final thank-you to Cauchy, who could not have known. In October of 1847 he was sixty years old, working out an orbital problem, and he wrote down two and a half pages explaining how to minimize a function by walking downhill. He did not publish a more developed treatment. He moved on to other problems. He died in 1857. For a hundred years his method sat in the *Comptes Rendus* of the French Academy, occasionally cited, mostly forgotten.

Then a man named Frank Rosenblatt at Cornell wrote a paper in 1958 about a learning machine called the *perceptron*. He wanted to train it. He used gradient descent. (He called it *the delta rule* and didn't credit Cauchy; the literature was still recovering the citation.) The machine learned. Other people copied his trick. The trick worked on bigger problems. The trick is still working. The two and a half pages turn out to have been the foundation of everything.
