# A single bowl

A confession before we close. Two chapters ago, in *Linear regression*, the story ended with a closed-form solution. We minimized the sum of squared residuals and a formula fell out — $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$. We left it as if Gauss had simply solved the problem in 1809 and gone home, and the rest of statistics had followed.

That isn't quite the whole story. Gauss could solve it because the *shape of the loss surface* admitted a closed form. Now that we've spent a chapter staring at loss surfaces, we can say more precisely what that means.

Here is the SSE for a tiny synthetic dataset (twenty-two points generated from $y = 0.4 + 1.2 x + \varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ a little Gaussian noise), viewed as a function of the line's intercept $a$ and slope $b$. The heatmap shows the loss value on the $(a, b)$ plane. The vermilion $\times$ is the closed-form minimum.

Drag the small circle around. You can position $(a, b)$ anywhere. *There is no second minimum.* The whole surface is a single convex bowl, like the very first paraboloid we saw in act 1 — only ellipsoidal because the $x$ and $y$ axes scale differently. Whichever direction you move from the $\times$, the loss goes up. Whichever starting point you pick, vanilla gradient descent will eventually find the same minimum. There's no choice to make.

The reason the loss is shaped this way is that $\text{SSE}(a, b)$ is a *quadratic function* of $a$ and $b$. Quadratic functions of two variables are paraboloids — bowls. Bowls are convex. Convex functions have a unique minimum (or a flat valley of equivalent minima, in degenerate cases). And the minimum of a quadratic is the place where its gradient is zero. That equation — *set the gradient to zero* — was solvable by Gauss in 1809 with paper, ink, and the determinant rules he'd worked out for solving linear systems.

For ordinary least squares, then, *no gradient descent is needed*. The closed form *is* the answer to the optimization problem; it's just that the optimization problem is so easy you can solve it with algebra instead of climbing.

What about everything else?

*Logistic regression* (next chapter) has a loss surface that is still convex — it's a sum of log-sigmoid terms — but no closed form exists for the minimum. Gradient descent is required. The good news: the loss is convex, so any descent algorithm finds the global minimum.

*Neural networks* have non-convex loss surfaces. Multiple minima, saddles, the works. Gradient descent finds *a* minimum, not *the* minimum. (We saw this two acts ago.)

*Modern transformer models* have non-convex loss surfaces *in a billion dimensions*. They are trained by stochastic gradient descent — basically the algorithm in this chapter, with a small noise added by sampling random batches of data — and they find loss values that are good enough to produce extraordinary behavior. The fact that gradient descent *works at all* on these landscapes is one of the empirical miracles of modern machine learning. We don't have a fully satisfying theory of why. The picture you're looking at is the bowl. The picture from now on will be a stranger surface, but the algorithm is the same. We climb.
