# Roll long enough and the average will tell

When you average a large enough number of rolls of a fair die, the average lands at $3.5$.

That's not a wish. It's a theorem, and a famous one. Jakob Bernoulli, working in Basel in the years before his death in 1705, proved a version of it; his nephew Nicholas published it in 1713 as part of *Ars Conjectandi*, the same book that gave us the term "law of large numbers." Bernoulli called it his *theorema aureum* — the golden theorem — and he meant it. He'd been chasing the result for twenty years.

Here's the statement, in modern phrasing. Let $X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldots$ be independent samples from the same distribution, each with expected value $\mu$. Form the running sample mean

$$
\bar{X}_n = \frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}{n}.
$$

Then as $n$ grows large, $\bar{X}_n$ converges to $\mu$. In symbols: $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ as $n \to \infty$.

The horizontal vermilion line at $3.5$ is $\mu$ — the *true* expected value of the fair die, the thing you can compute on paper before rolling anything. The dark wobbling line is the running sample mean.

Two things are worth absorbing as you watch it.

The first is that the early wobbles are *wild*. With $n = 1$ the sample mean is whatever you rolled. With $n = 5$ it can sit at $2$ or at $5.4$ or anywhere in between. The variance of $\bar{X}_n$ shrinks like $1/n$, which means the *standard* deviation of $\bar{X}_n$ shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$ — slowly. To halve the wobble, you need four times the rolls. To shave it to a tenth, a hundred times the rolls. The convergence is real but it isn't fast.

The second is that the running mean's *destiny* is unambiguous. It doesn't wander around $3.5$ forever. It locks onto it. There is no scenario, no matter how many rolls you do, where the sample mean of a long enough run drifts to $4$ and stays there. The geometry of the problem forbids it. That's the content of Bernoulli's theorem.

This is why we trust statistical estimates at all. If you want to know the mean of some distribution and you can only sample it — say, the average lifetime of a brand of lightbulb, or the average click-through rate of an advert — the law of large numbers says you can estimate $\mu$ by averaging your samples, and the estimate gets arbitrarily close as the sample size grows. Without it, statistics is fortune-telling. With it, statistics is engineering.

There's a subtle point worth flagging. The theorem says the *long-run* sample mean converges. It says nothing about *one particular* run being close to $\mu$ at any *particular* $n$. The line in the canvas wobbles even at $n = 200$. Convergence is a statement about the limit. In practice we use it as a guide — "more samples is better" — and the rate of convergence ($1/\sqrt{n}$, governed by the variance) becomes the next thing to ask about.
