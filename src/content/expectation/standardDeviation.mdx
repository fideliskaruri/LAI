# Back to the right units

Variance has a problem you might already feel without being able to name. If $X$ is measured in metres, the variance is in *square metres*. If $X$ is measured in dollars, the variance is in *dollars squared* — a unit that does not correspond to anything you can carry in your pocket. Compute the variance of a daily stock return and you'll get a number in (percentage-of-portfolio)$^2$, which means nothing to a human who wants to know how much they might lose tomorrow.

The fix is, blissfully, just to take the square root. The *standard deviation* is

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X]} = \sqrt{E[(X-\mu)^2]}.
$$

By construction $\sigma$ is in the same units as $X$. Metres if $X$ is in metres, dollars if $X$ is in dollars. That's why every calculator output, every spreadsheet column, every statistical software package reports $\sigma$ — not variance — when asked "how spread is the data." Variance is what you compute with; standard deviation is what you compare against the data itself.

The visual on the right is the rest of the chapter's histogram with the standard deviation drawn as a horizontal vermilion bar centered on $\mu$ — a *width-bar*, extending one $\sigma$ to the left of the mean and one $\sigma$ to the right. The bar's units are pip-units, the same units as the outcomes. You can read off "a typical roll lands within $\sigma$ of the mean" by looking at the bar. Drag the histogram to make $\sigma$ grow or shrink and watch the width-bar respond directly.

A useful piece of folklore, and a bit of preview: for many naturally-occurring distributions, about *two-thirds* of the probability mass lies within $\pm \sigma$ of the mean, and about *ninety-five percent* within $\pm 2\sigma$. The exact figures hold for the normal (Gaussian) distribution; they're approximate for other shapes; they're not even close for very skewed or heavy-tailed ones. But as a rule of thumb — "most things are within a couple of standard deviations of their mean" — it's astonishingly useful.

The standard deviation is also what's hiding inside the standard error of the mean. The law of large numbers in the previous act told you the running sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ converges to $\mu$. The standard deviation of $\bar{X}_n$ — that is, the typical wobble of your estimate — is exactly $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$. So if a fair die has $\sigma \approx 1.71$ (which you can verify by dragging the bars back to a uniform distribution and reading the readout), then averaging $100$ rolls produces a sample mean whose typical deviation from $3.5$ is about $0.17$. Averaging $10{,}000$ rolls, about $0.017$. This is the engineering rule of thumb behind every "we need $N$ users in our A/B test" calculation.

Mean, variance, standard deviation. These three numbers — $\mu$, $\sigma^2$, $\sigma$ — are the *summary statistics* MML §6.4 has in mind when it tells you what to remember about a distribution. They are the spine of statistics: the centre, the spread, and the spread in honest units.
