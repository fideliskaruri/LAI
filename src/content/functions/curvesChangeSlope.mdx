# Curves don't sit still

Same setup as before. Two dots. A chord. But the function is now `f(x) = x²` — the parabola from earlier. Galileo's curve.

Drag either dot.

Notice what happens to the slope readout. It moves. Not just a little — wildly. Slide both dots to the right of the origin and the slope is positive and large. Slide them to the left and it's negative. Bring them close to the bottom of the bowl and it shrinks toward zero. Put one on each side of the origin and you get something in between, depending on how you arrange them.

For a linear function we got the same answer no matter where the dots sat. For this one, the answer is a different number for every pair of dots.

That's a problem.

If somebody asks you *what's the slope of x squared?* you can't answer with a single number. The slope is *steeper on the sides and shallower in the middle*. It depends on where you are. Saying "the slope of the parabola is two" or "the slope of the parabola is five" is meaningless unless you say *where*.

So we have to do something subtler. We have to talk about the slope *at a point*. Not between two points. At one.

This is where the trouble starts. The whole rise-over-run idea was a *ratio of differences* — vertical distance divided by horizontal distance. At a single point there is no horizontal distance. There's nowhere to go. The denominator is zero. We can't divide.

Galileo ran into the exact same wall, dressed in physics. He could measure the ball's distance and the total time. He could divide them and get an average speed. But the speed *at a single instant* — when the ratio would be a distance of nothing over a time of nothing — was always out of reach.

For nearly a century after Galileo, this was where smart people got stuck. *We can compute the slope between two points. We can't compute the slope at one point.* The two statements look like they should be related. Nobody could quite see how.

Then a 23-year-old Newton, sent home from Cambridge by the plague in 1666, started thinking about it. Independently, in Germany, Leibniz did the same thing in the 1670s. What they both noticed turns out to be the simplest move imaginable.
