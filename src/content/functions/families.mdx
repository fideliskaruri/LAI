# Same idea, very different shapes

The machine idea is the same — one number in, one number out. The *rule* can be anything you can write down. And the shape of the graph depends entirely on the rule.

Tap the tabs on the left. *Linear, quadratic, sine.*

The linear one — `f(x) = x` — is a straight line at forty-five degrees. Whatever you put in, you get back. Honest. Boring. The most useful function in applied math, partly because it's the easiest to reason about.

The quadratic — `f(x) = x²` — is a parabola. It's nearly flat near zero, where small numbers squared are even smaller numbers, and it shoots up at the edges, where large numbers squared are very large numbers. This is the shape of `position vs time` for a falling ball. Galileo's curve. You're looking at it.

The sine — `f(x) = sin x` — wobbles between minus one and plus one and never stops. This is the shape of a swinging pendulum's angle, the height of a wave, the AC voltage in the wall outlet. (I was a little surprised the first time someone told me a pendulum and a wall outlet had the same graph. They mostly do.)

Three rules. Three shapes. One framework.

Mathematicians sometimes call these *function families* — bigger groups that share something. Anything of the form `f(x) = a x + b` for some chosen numbers `a` and `b` is *linear*. Anything of the form `f(x) = a x² + b x + c` is *quadratic*. The faded ghosts on the canvas are there partly to remind you that you could keep adding rules forever — exponential, polynomial of any degree, logarithm — and each would give a different shape on the same axes.

You'll meet specific families throughout this site. Logistic regression uses a special S-shaped function called *sigmoid* to turn unbounded numbers into probabilities. Diffusion models use a *Gaussian* family to model noise. Even a transformer, at its heart, is a *long composition* of pretty boring functions.

But right now there's a question that's been waiting since the cold open. We have these shapes. The ball moves along one of them. What does it mean to ask *how steep* a function is — and is the answer the same everywhere?

Two of these curves answer that question differently. The next two acts are about why.
