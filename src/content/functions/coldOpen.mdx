# How fast, right now?

It's somewhere around 1604. Galileo Galilei is sliding bronze balls down a wooden ramp in Padua. He's nailing them to a groove, releasing them, and timing how long they take to reach marks he's scratched into the wood. He's doing this for years.

The thing he's trying to figure out is, he thinks, the simplest thing in the world. *How does a falling object move?*

Aristotle had said, for two thousand years, that heavier things fall faster. Galileo, watching balls roll, became sure that wasn't right. He noticed something else. If he marked the ramp at the place the ball reached after one beat, then again after two beats, then three — the distances came out in the proportion 1, 4, 9, 16. Squares. The ball wasn't moving at a steady speed. It was speeding up.

Watch the ball on the left.

The ramp is the same Galileo's was. The tick marks at 1, 4, 9, 16 are his. The ball gets to the first tick slowly, then crosses the gap to the fourth tick in the same amount of time, then to the ninth, then to the sixteenth. Equal times — wildly unequal distances.

Which means at every instant the ball has a different speed. Here's the problem he never quite cracked: *what's the speed right now?* Not the average speed over the last second. Not the average over the next tenth of a second. *Right now.*

You can measure where the ball is. You can measure how much time has passed. The ratio is an average. To get the speed at a single instant, you'd need to measure a distance over no time at all, which is zero divided by zero, which is nothing.

It took eighty more years and two of the strangest minds in the history of science — Newton, who didn't publish; Leibniz, who did — to find the trick. The trick is the whole subject called *calculus*, and this chapter is the warm-up to it.

Before we can talk about *change*, we need to be very clear about the thing that's changing. We need to be clear about *functions*.
