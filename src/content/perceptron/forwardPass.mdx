# A line in the plane, a verdict on each side

Forget photocells for a moment. We're working in two dimensions because everything important about the perceptron happens in two dimensions; the four-hundred-dimensional version is identical, just with more numbers.

On the left panel is a feature space &mdash; the (x, y) plane &mdash; with a vermilion line drawn across it. That line is the boundary

$$
w_1 x + w_2 y + b \;=\; 0
$$

with three particular numbers for w₁, w₂, b that I've picked for the picture. (They don't matter; pick others and the line moves.) The two half-planes on either side of the line are tinted faintly: red on one side, blue on the other. Anywhere a point lands in the red half, the perceptron outputs 1. Anywhere in the blue half, 0. That is the geometry of a linear classifier in one sentence.

Now drag the point. Or use Tab and arrow keys; Shift-arrow steps faster. As you slide the point around, watch the right panel: that's the *neuron diagram*. The two input nodes x and y receive your point's coordinates. The edges multiply by the weights w₁ and w₂. The summing junction Σ adds them up, along with the bias b, into one real number z. The step gate at the end fires if z ≥ 0 and emits 1, otherwise 0. That number sits at the output.

The interesting moment is the *crossing*. Drag the point slowly until it hits the line. At the exact instant the point crosses the boundary, z passes through zero, and the step gate flips its output from 0 to 1 (or 1 to 0, depending on which way you crossed). The screen reader will announce the flip. There is no in-between. No "almost". No 0.5. The output is what mathematicians call *discontinuous in the inputs* &mdash; a small movement of the point can produce a jump from 0 to 1, but only when it happens to cross the line. Move parallel to the line and nothing changes. Move perpendicular and you cross it the fastest.

This is the perceptron's whole personality. *I draw a line. I tell you which side you're on. I don't tell you how close to the line you are.*

A few mechanical observations that will matter later:

*The pre-activation z is a signed distance.* Far from the line on the red side, z is large and positive. Far on the blue side, z is large and negative. Right on the line, z = 0. (The magnitude of z is the distance to the line times the length of the weight vector √(w₁² + w₂²). We'll exploit this in the SVM chapter to build a *margin*. The perceptron doesn't care about margin &mdash; it only cares about which side &mdash; but the signed distance is sitting right there.)

*The weight vector (w₁, w₂) is perpendicular to the line.* Geometrically, w is the *normal* of the boundary. It points from the blue half into the red half. Multiplying x by w and adding b gives you a signed projection onto that normal direction.

*The bias b is a translation.* Increase b and the line slides toward the blue half (the red half grows). Decrease b and the line slides the other way. Without a bias, the line would always pass through the origin &mdash; geometrically inflexible, mathematically lazy, almost never what you want.

That is the forward pass. Two multiplications, one addition, one threshold. The Mark I did this same thing four hundred times in parallel and got to the same answer. In a modern transformer it happens trillions of times. The shape is identical.

What's missing is *learning*. So far we've assumed the weights w₁, w₂, b are given. In the next act we'll let the machine pick them.
