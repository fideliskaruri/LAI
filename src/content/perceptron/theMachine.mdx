# What was in the box

It helps to look at the literal architecture &mdash; the diagram on the left is the one Rosenblatt drew in his 1958 Cornell technical report &mdash; because once you've seen it you can never again be surprised by what a "neuron" is. There are three stages.

*One. The retina.* A grid of photocells. Pixels, really. In the Mark I there were four hundred of them, arranged twenty-by-twenty. Shine a triangle in front of the array; some cells light up, others stay dark. Each cell emits a number between 0 and 1 representing how much light hit it. That's the *input vector* x. Four hundred numbers.

*Two. The association units (A-units).* Each A-unit is wired to a small random subset of the retina cells &mdash; about ten per A-unit in the Mark I &mdash; and applies a fixed (un-trainable) function to the cells it sees. Rosenblatt thought of these as primitive feature detectors, the way a biological retina has cells tuned to edges or moving dots. There were about a thousand A-units. None of them learned. They were the fixed wiring of the front end. (The interesting thing about A-units is that, decades later, people working on convolutional networks would put learnable feature detectors here and that's what made vision work. But that's chapter 19.)

*Three. The response unit.* This was the trainable part. It took the outputs of the A-units &mdash; call them a₁, a₂, …, aₙ &mdash; and computed one weighted sum:

$$
z = w_1 a_1 + w_2 a_2 + \cdots + w_n a_n + b
$$

Then it asked: is z bigger than zero? If yes, the response unit fired and the machine said *one class*. If no, it stayed silent and the machine said *the other class*. That's the **step function**. The hard threshold. The little staircase glyph in the diagram on the right.

The weights w₁, w₂, …, wₙ &mdash; one per A-unit &mdash; were stored physically. Each was the dial setting of a small electric motor driving a potentiometer. When the learning algorithm wanted to "adjust w₃", a motor whirred and a knob turned. The whole rack of motors clacked away during training. (Cornell's facilities staff, by all accounts, were not enthusiastic.)

If we strip away the photocells and the motors and the A-units &mdash; which are just a fixed transform from raw pixels to features &mdash; what's left is the modern equation on the right pane:

$$
\hat y \;=\; \operatorname{step}(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b)
$$

That's the perceptron. A vector dot product. A bias. A sign. Three operations, in order. Everything we do from here is variations on this picture.

Notice in particular what this *is not*: it is not differentiable. The step function has a jump at z = 0; there is no slope to follow there. We cannot do gradient descent on this directly. (Logistic regression, the previous chapter, solved this by replacing step with sigmoid &mdash; the smooth cousin. We'll need a different trick.) And it has no notion of *probability*. The output is a hard 0 or 1; no confidence, no "almost", nothing in between. It's a yes or it's a no.

For all that, this little machine could learn. The next act shows how.
