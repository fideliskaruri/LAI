# Capable was the easy part

On the left is the 2020s constellation of language models, plotted by year on the x-axis and parameter count on the y-axis. Twelve dots. **GPT-3** in June 2020 at 175 billion parameters &mdash; the first model big enough for in-context learning to be unmistakable. **Codex** in 2021, an OpenAI specialisation for code that became GitHub Copilot. **Chinchilla** from DeepMind in March 2022 at 70 billion parameters &mdash; the moment we learned we had been overgrowing and underfeeding our models. **PaLM** from Google later that year at 540 billion. **ChatGPT**, the public release of an instruction-tuned, RLHF-aligned descendant of GPT-3, on the evening of **November 30, 2022**. *That dot*, in the lower-right region of the plot, is the one most people remember; it's the dot that made the rest of the constellation legible to the public. **GPT-4** in **March 2023** at roughly a trillion parameters, mixture-of-experts. **Claude 1** from Anthropic, also March 2023. **Llama 2** from Meta in July 2023, the first credible open-weights model at the scale where the emergence curves had begun to wake up. **Gemini 1** from Google in December 2023. **Claude 3** in early 2024. **Llama 3** in 2024, at 405 billion parameters and open weights. **GPT-4o** later in 2024, omni-modal. The dots keep arriving.

If you stare at the chart long enough you notice two things. The first is that the y-axis has a ceiling, and we are approaching it. The frontier models of 2024 are around a trillion parameters; pre-training on the open internet uses about *fifteen trillion tokens*; the compute budget is in the hundreds of millions of dollars per run. None of those numbers can grow by another factor of ten without running into hard limits &mdash; on the amount of text on Earth, on the world supply of high-end GPUs, on the willingness of capital markets to write checks of that size. The Kaplan/Chinchilla curves remain smooth, but the *practical* curve of "how much bigger can we make the next one" is leveling off. The second thing you notice is that the *dates* are getting closer together. The gap between GPT-3 and GPT-4 was thirty-two months. The gap between GPT-4 and GPT-4o was fourteen. The frontier is *iterating faster as it scales more cautiously*, with much of the gain coming from better data, better training recipes, better alignment, and better inference tricks rather than pure parameter count.

This is the right place to stop, because the next chapter is about the part of this story we deliberately left out. The base models of pre-training are *capable*; they have all the knowledge that fifteen trillion tokens of internet text can press into a trillion parameters, and they can be prompted, with few-shot examples, to do nearly any natural-language task. But they are *not useful in the way a chatbot is useful*. They will riff, dodge, list related questions, slip into the genre of the corpus. They will, if you let them, say things you do not want them to say &mdash; harmful things, false things, weird things &mdash; because all of those are present, somewhere, in their training data. The base model is a faithful compression of the internet's tendencies, and the internet is many things at once.

The turn from *capable* to *useful* is the next chapter. Its technical name is **RLHF** &mdash; *reinforcement learning from human feedback* &mdash; and its trick is to collect millions of human judgements about which of two model outputs is better, train a *reward model* on those judgements, and then fine-tune the language model to *maximise the reward* using reinforcement learning. The recipe was published by Long Ouyang and the InstructGPT team at OpenAI in March 2022, eight months before ChatGPT shipped. Without it, what shipped that November would have been an academic curiosity; with it, it was the fastest-growing consumer application in history, hitting a hundred million monthly users in roughly two months.

The thread the next chapter pulls. Pre-training gave us a model. Scaling gave us emergent capabilities. Instruction-tuning narrowed the model's response genre to "helpful." But the gap from helpful-by-default to actually-helpful-to-this-person-in-this-context is the gap that human feedback is asked to close. The base model *knows* enough; what it needs to learn is what *you* want.

We will leave you here, at the end of pre-training. The next chapter is about the part where the model meets the human and learns to behave.
