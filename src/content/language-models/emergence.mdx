# Flat, flat, flat — then a switch flips

Drag the vermilion slider on the left from left to right. Six task-accuracy curves rise in front of you, and the order in which they rise is part of the teaching. *Text completion* turns on first &mdash; around a hundred million parameters, the model can produce coherent English. *Basic question answering* turns on next, somewhere in the low billions; a one-billion-parameter model can tell you Paris is the capital of France but is shaky on multi-step math. Around ten billion parameters, *instruction following* begins to work &mdash; the model starts treating "explain X to Y" as an instruction rather than as a prompt to be completed. Then, somewhere around fifty to a hundred billion parameters, *multi-step arithmetic* and *chain-of-thought reasoning* abruptly become possible; before that scale, the model is nearly random on these tasks. By the time you reach the right edge of the slider, around a trillion parameters, the model is doing things &mdash; sustained tool use, agentic planning &mdash; that no one knew it could do until they sat down with a model that large and asked.

The phenomenon has a name: **emergence**. The original observation, formalised in a 2022 paper from Jason Wei, Yi Tay, and colleagues, is that some capabilities of language models are *not* smooth functions of scale &mdash; they appear sharply, near a threshold, in a way that is invisible from below. You train a ten-billion-parameter model and watch its arithmetic accuracy crawl along at random-chance. You scale it to a hundred billion parameters and the arithmetic accuracy goes from a few percent to fifty percent in one model jump. There was no curve to extrapolate; there was a *floor*, and then there was a *ramp*. The phase transition was hiding in the loss curve, masked by the averaging across all tokens of the corpus &mdash; the loss kept falling smoothly the whole time. It was just falling in a particular way that, past a certain scale, *enabled* a qualitatively new behaviour on a particular sub-task.

This is the part of the chapter where the reasonable practitioner gets nervous. The whole pitch of the previous act was *predictability* &mdash; Kaplan's smooth curves, Chinchilla's rule, "we can budget for the next decimal place." Emergence cuts against that. It says: yes, the loss is smooth, but the *behaviour* of the model isn't. Some abilities that you might want &mdash; arithmetic, reasoning, instruction following &mdash; might be invisible at a smaller scale and obvious at a larger one. You cannot test for them on the small model and infer that they'll be present on the large one. You have to *train the large model and find out*. This is exhilarating if you have the capital and terrifying if you don't.

A few honest caveats. First, *not all* claimed emergent behaviours have held up under careful re-measurement; a paper from Rylan Schaeffer and colleagues in 2023, *Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?*, argued that some of the sharp transitions are artefacts of the metric, and that a different metric on the same tasks would show smooth improvement at all scales. The truth is somewhere in between: the loss is smooth, *some* downstream metrics are sharp, and there is an honest argument about which side of the line each particular ability sits on. Second, the threshold scale for each ability is not magical &mdash; it depends on the model architecture, the training data, the prompting strategy, and the evaluation. It is not the case that "intelligence emerges at 100B parameters"; it's the case that a particular benchmark measuring a particular skill has its midpoint, on a particular model family, at roughly that scale. Third, the model is not learning the *concept* of arithmetic when arithmetic accuracy rises; it is, presumably, learning representations of multi-step structure that, *combined* with the residual stream's positional bookkeeping, *combined* with the right kind of prompting, are sufficient to push the accuracy up. The model is still doing next-token prediction. The architecture is still a transformer. The training objective is still the cross-entropy you saw two acts ago. The change is internal, and we don't have a clean account of *exactly what changes inside the network* during the phase transition.

The right pane lists what each scale tier feels like to a user. Sub-billion: pattern matching, occasional cleverness, no real instructions. Tens of billions: useful conversation, light coding, simple Q & A. Hundred billions: instruction following, chain-of-thought reasoning, code generation. Trillion: agentic behaviour, multi-step planning, tool use. The list will be wrong by 2026. It is *already* wrong &mdash; some of these capabilities have started to appear at smaller scales as architecture and training-data choices improve. But the ordering &mdash; completion before Q & A, Q & A before instructions, instructions before reasoning, reasoning before agency &mdash; has been remarkably durable. You always seem to need the prior abilities before the next ones become reachable.

This is the chapter's strangest fact. We have a training objective that is plainly insufficient for what we want the model to do &mdash; we want it to follow instructions, but we are training it to predict next tokens; we want it to do arithmetic, but the corpus has only token statistics &mdash; and yet, at enough scale, instructions and arithmetic *fall out for free*. Or close to free. We're going to need one more chapter to make them really useful; that's where alignment comes in. But the raw capability, the *potential* to follow instructions, *is already there in the base model*. The next act shows the smallest, oldest way of getting it to manifest, with no extra training at all.
