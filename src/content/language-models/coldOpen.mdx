# The bigram model and its descendants

On the left is a facsimile of the title page of *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, by Claude Shannon, which ran in the *Bell System Technical Journal* in **July and October 1948**, across two issues, occupying nearly eighty pages of dense mathematics. The vermilion box highlights the passage where Shannon, with no computer and no corpus to speak of, hand-built what would later be called an *n-gram language model* by leafing through a copy of Jefferson the Virginian, tallying which letters followed which other letters, and using the tallies as transition probabilities of a Markov chain. He drew samples from it by hand. He printed the samples in the paper. They read like the gibberish in the vermilion box: *IN NO IST LAT WHEY CRATICT FROURE BIRS GROCID PONDENOME OF DEMONSTURES.* On the right is the seventy-four years of work that followed.

Three things to notice in advance. The first is that Shannon's gibberish is not random. If you look at it carefully you'll see that two-letter sequences which English actually uses &mdash; ST, TH, ER, RE &mdash; appear; sequences which English never uses &mdash; QZ, ZX, JK &mdash; don't. The bigram statistics, just the bigram statistics, are already doing real work. The second is that Shannon himself never thought of this as a model of *meaning*. He thought of it as a model of *redundancy* &mdash; an engineering exercise in figuring out how compressible the English language was, so the telephone company could budget more sensibly for trunk-line capacity. The third is the gap between him and us. From the Bell Labs hand-tallied bigram in 1948 to ChatGPT going live to the public on the evening of **Wednesday, November 30, 2022** is seventy-four years and four months. Most of that time was spent on the same problem Shannon stated &mdash; *given the previous N tokens, what is the probability of the next?*

The timeline on the right is the genealogy. **1948**: Shannon's n-grams &mdash; transition probabilities between letters, then between words, computed by hand from a single book. **1992**: Peter Brown and his colleagues at IBM Research publish *Class-Based n-gram Models of Natural Language* in *Computational Linguistics*, grouping words into clusters so that statistics generalise across rare words. The IBM team takes the work further in **1993** with what become known as the **IBM translation models** &mdash; the same n-gram machinery, now applied to bilingual corpora and used to align French and English sentences word-by-word. SRI-LM, the open-source toolkit that lets every academic in the field play with smoothed n-grams, arrives in the **late 1990s**. **2003** is the first vertex of the modern era: Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent, and Christian Jauvin publish *A Neural Probabilistic Language Model* in *JMLR* &mdash; the first neural net trained to do what Shannon's chain had done, but with continuous word representations that *generalise*. **2013**, the chapter we're three back from: Tomáš Mikolov's word2vec turns word meaning into geometry. **2019**: GPT-2 from OpenAI shows that the same simple objective &mdash; predict the next token &mdash; produces eerie, fluent, structured English at scale. **November 30, 2022**: ChatGPT.

You can stare at this list for a while and ask the obvious question. What changed? Why did seventy-four years of patient incremental work suddenly inflect, somewhere between 2017 and 2022, into systems that the front page of *The New York Times* would care about? The answer is the chapter you're holding. It has three parts. The first is the recognition that the *task* &mdash; predict the next token &mdash; was always the right task. The second is the realisation that this task, repeated billions of times on a large corpus, produces a model with surprisingly broad knowledge of the world. The third is the discovery that *scale* &mdash; more parameters, more data, more compute &mdash; produces *qualitatively new behaviour*, not just incrementally better completions. These three together are what people mean by *language models* in the modern sense. We'll work through them act by act.

A word on jargon, before we go further. In this chapter "model" means a function from a token sequence to a probability distribution over the next token. "Pre-training" means training that function on a big corpus by maximising the log-probability of the actual next token at every position. "Token" means a chunk of text, usually a word-piece in modern systems; for this chapter, you can think of it as a word and not lose much. "Cross-entropy" is the loss the training optimises &mdash; the negative log-probability the model assigned to the true next token, averaged over the corpus. *Bits per token* is the same quantity, divided by log(2). All of these will appear once and then we'll go quiet about them; the chapter is interested in the picture, not the bookkeeping.

The story begins where Shannon began &mdash; with one blank and a handful of choices for what to put in it. Turn the page.
