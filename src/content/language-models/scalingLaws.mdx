# A power law, on log-log paper

Drag the vermilion knob on the left. It moves along a curve that looks &mdash; once you've stared at it for a few seconds &mdash; conspicuously like a straight line. That's because it *is*, more or less, a straight line, and the axes are *both logarithmic*. The thing on display is the headline figure of Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, and colleagues at OpenAI, in a paper titled *Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models*, posted to arXiv on **January 23, 2020**. The paper's claim is that, when you train language models of different sizes on the same data with the same recipe, the cross-entropy loss is well-approximated by a *power law in the number of parameters N*:

$$
L(N) \;\approx\; \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \quad \alpha_N \approx 0.076.
$$

On log-log paper, a power law is a straight line of slope *−α*. So the line you're dragging the knob along is the predicted loss as you grow the model from a hundred million parameters to ten trillion, all else held equal. Doubling the model size buys you a constant decrement of the loss, in bits per token. The exponent is small &mdash; about 0.076 in Kaplan's fit &mdash; which means the gains from scale are real but not free. Going from a billion parameters to a trillion (three orders of magnitude) buys you somewhere around a 0.6× reduction in the loss-minus-floor; not a hundred times better, not even ten times better, but still better, *reliably*. The reliability is the point.

Kaplan and McCandlish weren't the first to notice that bigger language models were better. They were the first to *fit a clean functional form to it*, on tens of independent training runs spanning seven orders of magnitude in model size and compute, and show that the form held across all of them. The paper has three scaling laws, one for each of {parameters, data, compute}; all three are power laws, all three with single-digit-percent exponents, all three with the same dispiriting and exhilarating implication: *the next decimal place is paid for in dollars, predictably*. You can sit in 2020 and look at the slope and know what GPT-4 will cost to train two years before anyone has trained it. You can budget. You can plan. You can &mdash; if you have the capital &mdash; just *bid for the next data point on the curve*, knowing exactly what it will get you. This is what people mean when they say modern AI is a *capital-intensive industry*.

A subtlety the curve hides. Kaplan's original 2020 paper made a recommendation about how to *allocate compute* between model size and training tokens: roughly, that you should grow the model faster than the data, that bigger-and-undertrained beat smaller-and-overtrained. **Two years later**, in March 2022, Jordan Hoffmann, Sebastian Borgeaud, and colleagues at DeepMind posted *Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models* &mdash; the **Chinchilla** paper &mdash; and overturned the Kaplan allocation. Their experiment trained models of many sizes for many durations and showed that, holding compute fixed, the *optimum* was when the model and the data scaled at roughly equal rates: about *twenty training tokens per parameter*. The previous generation of frontier models (GPT-3, Gopher, Megatron-Turing) had been trained on far fewer than 20 tokens per parameter; they were, in the Chinchilla framing, *overgrown and underfed*. The right pane of the canvas takes the Chinchilla rule at face value: at every model size you drag to, it tells you how many training tokens that model should be fed. *D ≈ 20 · N*. At a billion parameters, you want twenty billion training tokens. At a hundred billion, two trillion. At a trillion, twenty trillion &mdash; about the size of the entire English internet, and roughly the size of the data behind GPT-4.

It is worth dwelling on what *both* papers got right. They both noticed that the loss curve is smooth, that it doesn't have plateaus you can lose money trying to climb past, and that it generalises across model sizes spanning seven orders of magnitude. They both gave the field a *fit*, a recipe for prediction, that you could disagree with the constants of but not the form of. The constants improved with Chinchilla; the *idea* was Kaplan's; the *consequence* was that the field went from "let's see if a bigger model helps" to "let's price the next training run." The compute readout on the right pane is the rough order of magnitude of the FLOPs needed to train this model at Chinchilla's allocation. Modern frontier models like GPT-4 sit in the *exaFLOP* range &mdash; meaning, very roughly, that even if you had every consumer GPU on Earth working in unison, the training run would still take weeks.

This is also where the chapter gets uncomfortable, briefly, for the working ML engineer. If the loss curve is *smooth*, and the recipe is *known*, then where are the surprises supposed to come from? The next act answers that question. Some of them, it turns out, come from the curve itself getting cute when you look at it through the right lens.
