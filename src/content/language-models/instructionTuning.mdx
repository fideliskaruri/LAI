# The same prompt, two very different models

Look at the two columns on the right. The prompt, sent to both, is *"Explain photosynthesis to a 10-year-old."* The left column is a base model's response. The right column is the instruction-tuned model's response. The base model treats the prompt as the first line of a longer document &mdash; perhaps a textbook chapter, perhaps a Stack Exchange thread, perhaps a question-bank for a biology teacher. It produces a list of *related questions*. Explain photosynthesis to a 12-year-old. Explain mitosis to a 10-year-old. Explain the water cycle to a 10-year-old. *— from Common Biology Questions, vol. 3.* The base model is doing exactly what we trained it to do: predict what kind of text usually follows a sentence shaped like that one. And it has decided, correctly, that what usually follows is a list of similar questions, because that pattern is everywhere in its training data.

The instruction-tuned model, given the same prompt, writes: *"Plants are like tiny solar-powered kitchens. They take three free ingredients — sunlight, water, and the carbon dioxide you breathe out — and use them to make sugar, which is their food. The leftover bit is oxygen, which they puff back into the air. That is why a forest feels easier to breathe in."* It *answers the question.* It picks a metaphor a child would understand. It uses short sentences. It returns oxygen to the air at the end. *It does what it was asked to do.* And the only difference between the two models is *a small training run on top of the base model*, of a few thousand to a few hundred thousand examples of the form "[instruction] → [helpful response]."

That training run is **instruction tuning**, sometimes called supervised fine-tuning or SFT. The recipe is one of the simplest in modern ML: take the base model. Collect a dataset of paired (instruction, ideal response) examples; a few thousand will do, though more is better. Continue training the model on this dataset, using the same next-token prediction loss as pre-training, for a small number of epochs. That's it. There is no new architecture, no new objective, no new tricks. The model's weights move a little &mdash; less than during pre-training, because the dataset is so much smaller &mdash; and the result is a model that *interprets prompts as instructions rather than as documents to complete*. The base model's vast latent knowledge is unchanged; what changes is the format of the model's output. The instruction-tuned model, asked about photosynthesis, now produces an answer-shaped response rather than a question-list-shaped response. It is still doing next-token prediction. It is just predicting next tokens *in the genre of "helpful answer"*.

Two early-2022 papers made this widely understood. *Fine-tuned Language Models are Zero-Shot Learners* (FLAN) from Jason Wei and colleagues at Google showed that you could fine-tune a base model on a *collection* of NLP tasks reformatted as instructions, and the resulting model would generalise to instructions it had never seen. *Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback* (the InstructGPT paper, March 2022) from Long Ouyang and colleagues at OpenAI introduced the recipe that would later become ChatGPT: pre-train a base model, then *supervised fine-tune* on human-written demonstrations of helpful responses, then go further with reinforcement learning (the subject of the next chapter). The supervised fine-tuning step alone &mdash; just the instruction-tuning &mdash; was enough to produce a model that, in human evaluations, was *preferred* to the base model the vast majority of the time, even when the base model was many times larger.

A small philosophical aside, because this is where the chapter's central claim crystallises. The base model is, in some sense, a *world model* &mdash; a compressed statistical representation of the corpus it was trained on, with surprising depth and breadth, capable of being prompted to do almost anything. But it is *not aligned with what you actually want*. It will, given a chance, complete your prompt the way the internet tends to. The internet's average behaviour, when handed a question, is not to answer it &mdash; it is to riff, to mock, to ask a meta-question, to post a meme, to list related questions, to begin a Reddit-thread-of-replies. The base model has all of these behaviours latent in it because all of them are well-represented in its training data. Instruction tuning is the act of *narrowing the model's response distribution* to the small slice of the corpus that looks like helpful answers. The model can still produce the other genres; it has just been gently pushed to default to the helpful-answer one.

This is the *minimum viable alignment*. It is supervised, not reinforcement; it uses a small amount of clean data, not millions of preference rankings. It produces a model that is dramatically more useful than the base model and dramatically less useful than what a modern chatbot ships with. The remaining gap &mdash; the gap between an instruction-tuned model and a genuinely helpful one &mdash; is what the next chapter, RLHF, exists to close. But the move on display here is the load-bearing one: *take the base model, fine-tune it on a small dataset of helpful responses, and the model starts being helpful.* It is the single most cost-effective intervention in modern ML &mdash; perhaps a thousand-times reduction in training cost over pre-training, perhaps a ten-times improvement in user-facing utility. It is the trick that turned GPT-3, in 2020, from a research curiosity into ChatGPT, in November 2022.

We end the chapter, then, with two models. The base model knows a great deal and listens to no one. The instruction-tuned model knows the same things and answers your question. The next chapter is about the further step that produced the system you might have used today.
