# A solar-system worth of words

Tap *play* on the left. The cursor advances one word at a time across a slab of corpus text &mdash; a mixture of Wikipedia-ish prose, a snippet of Python, a SQL query, a couplet of poetry, a bit of HTML, a passage of recipe French. As the cursor moves, on the right a vermilion dot slides down the cross-entropy loss curve. Every step, the model is doing the same thing: looking at the words before the cursor, computing a probability distribution over what should come next, comparing its prediction to the word actually there, and quietly absorbing the gradient. *Predict next token. Compare. Update. Predict next token. Compare. Update.* For a long time. Then for a longer time. Then for a very long time.

This is **pre-training**, and it is the load-bearing trick of every modern language model. The thing on the left, conceptually, is a slice of "the internet" plus some books plus some code. In real systems, the training corpus is on the order of a *trillion words*, which is roughly all the text Google ever indexed circa 2010, minus the spam. The cursor moves through it once or twice or sometimes a handful of times, and the model's parameters are nudged at every position to make the actual next word a bit more probable. Over the course of pre-training the model sees something like a hundred trillion training examples, where each example is *(context, next-word)*. The same parameters get nudged by every one. Each nudge is small. The number of nudges is enormous. *The result is a system that has, in some loose sense, read more text than any human ever could.*

The loss curve on the right is what falls during this process. The y-axis is **bits per token** &mdash; the average number of bits the model would need, on a hypothetical compression scheme, to encode the next word given the previous ones. The x-axis is the number of training tokens seen, on a log scale. The curve is famously *smooth*. There is no aha moment. There are no plateaus. The loss falls, predictably and continuously, by roughly a constant factor with every doubling of training data. Past a certain point the curve bends &mdash; we'll come back to that in the next act when we look at Kaplan's scaling laws &mdash; but for the bulk of pre-training, the picture is what you see: a long, gentle slide downward. The dotted vermilion line near the bottom is the *irreducible loss* &mdash; the entropy of natural English text itself, the floor beneath which no model can go because the next word in a sentence often genuinely *isn't* uniquely determined by what came before. Shannon, in 1950, estimated this floor experimentally by having human subjects play a guessing game on English text and measuring how often they were right; his answer was *around 1 bit per character*, or roughly 1.5 bits per token in modern accounting. Forty-five years of n-gram and neural-LM work brought us within a factor of three of that floor. The next twenty years brought us within a factor of one-and-a-half.

It's worth pausing on the *one-trick* part of "one trick, billions of times." A language model is *not* trained to do conversation, or to answer questions, or to write code, or to translate from French to English, or to summarise an email, or to perform any of the tasks people actually use them for. It is trained, exclusively, to do *next-word prediction*. The reason it ends up able to do all those other things is that doing them well is *useful for the next-word prediction loss*. To predict the next word in a Stack Overflow answer, you must know some Python. To predict the next word in a French recipe, you must know some French. To predict the next word after "the boiling point of water at sea level is," you must know some physics. The model isn't *taught* any of this; it picks it up as a free side-effect of trying to compress the corpus by another fraction of a bit. The general capabilities that emerge are the model's way of being *good at the one task it was actually asked to do*.

The corpus you're scrolling on the left is, of course, tiny &mdash; about a dozen lines. A real pre-training corpus is something like *fifteen trillion tokens*, drawn from a heterogeneous blend of Common Crawl web pages, books and Wikipedia, code from GitHub, scientific papers from arXiv, and curated specialty sources. Roughly speaking: a trillion tokens of Wikipedia-quality reference text, ten trillion tokens of mixed web crawl, half a trillion tokens of code. Modern pre-training runs take weeks to months on thousands of GPUs and cost on the order of tens of millions of dollars. They produce a single artefact &mdash; the **base model** &mdash; whose weights, on disk, are perhaps a terabyte. *That* artefact is what we'll spend the rest of this chapter examining.

When you stop the cursor in the middle of the slab on the left, the model is, at that moment, making a prediction. The prediction is the entire content of the model. It has no other state. There is no scratchpad. There is no thinking. There is only, at every position, a probability distribution over the next token, and the gradient signal that comes from comparing it to what was actually next. *That* is pre-training. The next act asks what happens to the loss curve, and to the model, when you scale this trick up.
