# Three examples, no training

On the left is a prompt textbox with three little examples in it: `fr: chien → en: dog`, `fr: chat → en: cat`, `fr: oiseau → en: ?`. On the right, the model's completion appears one token at a time: *en: bird.* The model has done no training on this task. No gradient has been computed. No weight has been updated. The base model that produced this output is the same base model from two acts ago &mdash; the one whose entire training was "predict the next token in some pile of web text." Tap the *swap* button on the left to switch to a different deck. The examples become `France → Paris, Japan → Tokyo, Brazil → ?`; the completion turns into *Brasília*. Swap again: `3 → 6, 7 → 14, 11 → ?`; the completion is *22*. The model's behaviour is changing dramatically &mdash; from French translator to capital-city lookup to doubling-arithmetic machine &mdash; and the *only thing that's changed is the prompt*.

This phenomenon has a name: **in-context learning**, sometimes shortened to ICL. It was first cleanly described by Tom Brown, Benjamin Mann, and the GPT-3 team in *Language Models are Few-Shot Learners*, the paper that introduced GPT-3 in **May 2020**. The paper's most quoted figure is a plot of accuracy on a battery of tasks, with model scale on the x-axis and *number of examples in the prompt* (zero, one, "few") as the lines. Zero-shot accuracy improves slowly with scale. One-shot accuracy improves faster. Few-shot accuracy improves fastest of all. By the time you get to GPT-3's 175 billion parameters, the few-shot curves are *miles* above the zero-shot ones &mdash; the model is, in some real operational sense, *using the examples in the prompt to do something that looks like learning.*

The slogan that captured this is *the prompt is the program*. Before in-context learning, if you wanted a language model to do a specialised task &mdash; translate French, classify sentiment, summarise an article &mdash; you had to fine-tune it. You collected a labelled dataset for the task, you ran a small training job that nudged the model's weights to do well on that task, and you ended up with a *specialised* model that was good at your task and worse at everything else. The model's behaviour was *baked into its weights*. In-context learning showed that, past a certain scale, you didn't need any of that. You could change the model's behaviour by changing the input. The weights stayed exactly as the base model's pre-training had left them. The task specification &mdash; "translate French to English by example" or "double the number" &mdash; was carried entirely by *the structure of the prompt*. You write a program for the model by *showing it three examples of what the program should do*.

It is hard to overstate how surprising this was at the time. A 175-billion-parameter model trained on next-token prediction *should not* be able to do this. There is no obvious mechanism in the training objective that says "learn how to do tasks specified by examples." And yet, past a certain scale, it falls out anyway. This is the most concrete example of *emergence* from the previous act &mdash; few-shot in-context learning is exactly the kind of capability that turns on sharply at scale, that's invisible at a billion parameters, and that's reliable at a hundred billion. Why does it work? The honest answer is that we don't fully know. The most popular explanation, due in part to Sewon Min and colleagues' 2022 paper *Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations*, is that the prompt's examples primarily serve to *evoke* a pre-existing pattern-matching ability latent in the model's pre-training &mdash; the examples teach the *format*, not the *content*. Other work suggests the model is doing something genuinely close to implicit gradient descent inside its forward pass. The mechanism is open research.

A practical note. In-context learning is *cheap*. It costs you the tokens in the prompt and the tokens of the completion, nothing more. There is no training run, no labelled data, no infrastructure beyond an API. It is also *brittle*: the model's behaviour is sensitive to the order of examples, the choice of separator, the exact format of the labels. There is a small, oddly poetic field of study called *prompt engineering* that catalogues these sensitivities and writes them up as guidelines. The guidelines are unreliable in their specifics and reliable in spirit &mdash; *the prompt matters, sometimes a lot, in ways that are not always intuitive*. Anyone who has worked with these models for any length of time has been surprised by the difference a comma or a "Q:" prefix can make.

The base model, after pre-training, is *already* a few-shot learner. It can be told what task to do by being shown a few examples of the task. It can be coaxed, with a clever prompt, to do chain-of-thought reasoning, to write code, to translate, to summarise. What it cannot reliably do is *follow a direct instruction*. The next act is about the small extra step that makes it do that &mdash; the step that turns a capable model into a useful one.
