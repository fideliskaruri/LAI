# The paper that ate the field

On the 12th of June 2017 a paper called *Attention Is All You Need* was posted to arXiv. The title was provocative on purpose. Eight authors at Google &mdash; six in Mountain View at Google Brain, two in Berlin at Google Research &mdash; were saying that the recurrent neural network, which had been the architectural backbone of natural language processing for thirty years, was unnecessary. You could throw the recurrence away, keep only the attention mechanism Dzmitry Bahdanau and his collaborators had bolted onto encoder-decoder RNNs three years earlier, stack the attention blocks deeper, and the resulting model would beat every previous translation system &mdash; while training faster, scaling better, and parallelising across hardware in a way recurrent nets fundamentally could not.

The eight authors were **Ashish Vaswani**, **Noam Shazeer**, **Niki Parmar**, **Jakob Uszkoreit**, **Llion Jones**, **Aidan N. Gomez**, **Lukasz Kaiser**, and **Illia Polosukhin**. The first six worked at Google Brain in Mountain View; Uszkoreit and Polosukhin worked at Google Research in Berlin. Aidan Gomez was an intern from the University of Toronto. The paper credits Noam Shazeer with substantial parts of the architecture; in private accounts, Shazeer is the person who proposed throwing the recurrence away. Eight months later the paper was presented at NeurIPS 2017 in Long Beach. Within a year, every modern language model on earth was a variant of it.

To feel how strange this was, you have to remember what the world looked like beforehand. The reigning architecture for sequence problems &mdash; translation, summarisation, speech, anything where the input came as a stream &mdash; was the **long short-term memory** network, invented by Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber in **1997** to fix the vanishing-gradient problem in plain RNNs. LSTMs had been the backbone of Google Translate since 2016. In 2014, Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals, and Quoc Le at Google had published *Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks*, which married two LSTMs &mdash; one to read the source sentence into a fixed vector, one to write the target sentence out of that vector &mdash; into the *encoder-decoder* architecture that defined neural machine translation for the next three years. Later that same year, Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio at Montreal proposed *attention* as a way to let the decoder look back at different parts of the source sentence at different output positions, rather than squeezing everything through one fixed bottleneck. Their paper appeared on arXiv in September 2014 and was published at ICLR 2015. Attention was widely seen as a useful bolt-on. Nobody, in 2014 or 2015 or even 2016, was suggesting you could throw the RNN itself away.

In 2017 the eight authors did. They kept attention; they dropped recurrence entirely; they used position information as an *additive feature* of the embedding so the model still knew word order; they stacked the resulting blocks six deep on each side of an encoder-decoder architecture; and they trained the result on WMT 2014 (the standard machine-translation benchmark of the day, mapping English to German). It beat the state of the art by about two points on the *BLEU* score &mdash; a number from 0 to 100 that measures how close a model's translation is to a human reference. It also trained in roughly twelve hours on eight P100 GPUs (a top-end research-grade graphics card from 2016 made for matrix-multiplication workloads) &mdash; one or two orders of magnitude faster than the recurrent baselines.

The paper itself is sixteen pages, including appendices. It is unusually clear. The architecture diagram on the second page &mdash; you have probably seen it &mdash; is now the most reproduced figure in machine learning. The architecture has one elegant central trick (multi-head scaled dot-product attention), one elegant central convenience (position is added, not encoded recurrently), and a handful of stabilising tricks for training deep stacks that we'll meet in later acts. Within months, **BERT** (Devlin and colleagues at Google AI, 2018) and **GPT** (Radford and colleagues at OpenAI, 2018) had each taken half the architecture &mdash; BERT kept the encoder, GPT kept the decoder &mdash; and used the result as a *pretraining backbone*. The pretraining-and-fine-tune paradigm followed in 2019. In 2020, **GPT-3** at 175 billion parameters (parameters being the model's learnable weights, the count of which is the single best predictor of capability in the 2020s) demonstrated that this architecture, scaled up by three orders of magnitude, exhibited capabilities nobody had predicted. On **November 30, 2022**, OpenAI released **ChatGPT** &mdash; a GPT-3.5-class transformer wrapped in a conversational interface &mdash; and a hundred million people tried it within two months. By 2024 the entire frontier of AI &mdash; Claude, Gemini, GPT-4, Llama &mdash; was transformer architectures.

This chapter is about that 2017 paper. We will not try to do justice to all sixteen of its pages. We will rebuild the architecture from the inside, one piece at a time: tokens to embeddings; positions added in as waves; the queries-and-keys-and-values that *are* attention; the eight heads that read in parallel; the feed-forward layer and the residual skip that make everything trainable; and the encoder-decoder pairing that translates English into French one token at a time. By the end you will know what the architecture diagram on page two means, and why the people who first saw it knew within five minutes that the field had just shifted.
