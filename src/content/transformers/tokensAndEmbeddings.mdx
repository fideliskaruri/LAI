# Words become numbers, and then numbers become geometry

Tap any of the six chips in the left pane. *The cat sat on the mat.* Six tokens, six chips, six tiny integers underneath. The right pane shows the same selection as an eight-bar chart: the embedding vector for that token. Drag your attention across the row of chips and the bars on the right shift in step.

A transformer does not know what *cat* is. It only knows that *cat* is integer 8415, that 8415 maps to a particular point in an eight-dimensional space (or in the real GPT-2, a 768-dimensional space; in GPT-3, 12,288 dimensions), and that this point is closer to the point for *dog* than to the point for *eleven*. Everything else &mdash; syntax, grammar, semantics, the entire reasoning capability we are about to spend the chapter building &mdash; is derived from geometric relationships in this vector space and from how those relationships get mixed together across positions in the sentence.

The first step is **tokenization**: the conversion from a string of characters into a sequence of integers. There are many ways to do this. The simplest is one integer per word, but that's wasteful and brittle &mdash; it cannot represent any word it has not seen before. The modern standard, used by GPT-2 onward, is **byte-pair encoding** (BPE), invented by Philip Gage as a compression algorithm in 1994 and adapted to neural machine translation by Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow, and Alexandra Birch in 2016. BPE starts with characters as the alphabet and then iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, building up a vocabulary of subword pieces. Common words ("the", " cat", " is") become single tokens. Rare words split into smaller pieces: in many tokenizers, *cybersecurity* would be three tokens, *unrecognisable* four. The leading space matters: " cat" with a space and "cat" without are *different tokens*, because they appear in different syntactic contexts. GPT-4's vocabulary is about a hundred thousand tokens. Llama's is about thirty thousand. The numbers underneath each chip on the left are not specifically realistic (they are illustrative) but they capture the structure: each token is an integer index into a giant lookup table.

The lookup table is the **embedding matrix**. Conceptually it is a matrix with one row per vocabulary token and one column per embedding dimension. To embed a token, you do nothing more than read out that token's row. In the picture, that lookup happens between the chip on the left and the bar chart on the right: the model sees the integer id, walks to that row of the embedding matrix, and produces the vector you see drawn. That vector is real-valued, dense, and (initially) random. During training, gradient descent shapes the embedding matrix so that tokens with similar grammatical and semantic roles end up with similar vectors. The Word Embeddings chapter, two back, showed exactly how this geometry forms &mdash; *king* &minus; *man* + *woman* arrives, after enough training, near *queen*. The same effect is what the transformer's embedding matrix produces.

The eight bars in the picture are also a lie of scale. GPT-2 small uses **768** dimensions per token. GPT-2 medium uses 1024. GPT-2 large uses 1280, GPT-2 xl uses 1600. GPT-3 uses 12,288. The numbers grow roughly with model capacity. The reason for hundreds rather than tens of dimensions is that the vector has to simultaneously encode many properties: part of speech, semantic category, register, sentiment, the fact that it appears after a determiner and before a verb. In a small space all of those would collide. In a high-dimensional space, almost any two random directions are nearly orthogonal, and the model can pack many independent features into the same vector without interference. This is a deep fact about high-dimensional geometry &mdash; the so-called *blessing of dimensionality* that comes from the same mathematics as the curse &mdash; and it is one of the reasons modern transformers work as well as they do.

What is *not* in the picture yet, and what we have to add next, is *position*. As things stand, the model knows what each token is, but it has no idea what order they appear in. *The cat sat on the mat* and *the mat sat on the cat* and *sat mat the on cat the* would all produce the same multiset of embedding vectors, and a model with no notion of position would treat them identically. That is the next act. It is also the most surprising piece of the entire architecture: how the transformer is told where in the sentence it is.
