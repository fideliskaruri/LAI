# What happens between the attention layers

Tap any of the six boxes on the left. The diagram lights up the chosen sub-component; the card on the right tells you what it does, roughly how many parameters live inside it, and why the architects put it there. Tap the **feed-forward** box first &mdash; that's the half of the block we haven't talked about yet.

We have spent four acts on attention and on the input plumbing that feeds it. But attention is not enough on its own. Attention *moves information across positions*: it lets each token gather context from the rest of the sentence. It does very little within a single position. If you stacked attention on attention on attention with nothing else between them, the resulting model would, mathematically, collapse to a single big attention layer &mdash; because composing linear operations gives another linear operation. The whole stack would be no more expressive than one layer. You need a **non-linearity** somewhere, and you need it applied *per position* (because the cross-position mixing is already the attention layer's job).

That non-linearity lives in the **feed-forward sub-block**, the half of the transformer that gets the least poetry written about it. It is structurally simple: each token's vector is sent through a two-layer multilayer perceptron. The first layer projects from *d* dimensions up to *4d* dimensions and applies a ReLU. The second layer projects back down from *4d* to *d*. The same MLP weights are used at every position, but each position's vector goes through independently &mdash; there is no cross-position interaction in the FFN. The expansion factor of 4 is a convention that has stayed remarkably stable; GPT-2, GPT-3, Llama all use it.

The FFN looks unglamorous. It is, in fact, where *most of the parameters* of a transformer live. With *d* = 768 in GPT-2 small, each FFN sub-block has roughly 4.7 million parameters &mdash; about two-thirds of every transformer block's total. By the time you reach GPT-3 with *d* = 12,288 and 96 layers, the FFN sub-blocks alone account for over 100 billion parameters. The 2017 paper has very little to say about why this expansion-and-contraction shape works; the empirical answer, ten years later, is that this is where the model stores *facts*. A line of mechanistic-interpretability research starting around 2020 (Geva, Schuster, Berant, and Levy; Anthropic's work on transformer circuits) has shown that individual neurons in FFN layers correspond to specific learned features &mdash; "this concept", "this kind of grammar", "this kind of named entity". Attention shuffles tokens around; the FFN *thinks about* them.

The other two pieces in the diagram are the **residual connections** (drawn as the dashed loops on the right) and the **add+norm** stages they feed into. These are the architectural plumbing that makes deep transformer stacks trainable.

The residual connection is a borrowing from computer vision. In 2015 Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren, and Jian Sun at Microsoft Research published *Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition* &mdash; the **ResNet** paper &mdash; which showed that you could train 50, 100, even 1000-layer convolutional networks if every layer's output was *added to its input* via a skip connection rather than replacing it. The mechanism is gradient: gradients flow backward through the skip connection essentially untouched, so even very deep networks can be trained without their gradient signal dying out. The transformer adopts the same trick. After each sub-layer &mdash; attention, then feed-forward &mdash; the sub-layer's output is *added* to its input. The model never has to fully reconstruct the input; it only has to *modify* it.

The accompanying **LayerNorm** was published the same year (2016) by Jimmy Lei Ba, Jamie Ryan Kiros, and Geoffrey Hinton. LayerNorm normalises the activations *within* a single example, computing a per-token mean and variance and rescaling. (BatchNorm, the older cousin, normalises across the batch, which works for vision but not for variable-length text. LayerNorm was invented precisely for the recurrent-sequence case.) In the 2017 paper, LayerNorm appears *after* the residual addition; in most modern transformers (GPT-2 onward, sometimes called "pre-norm"), it appears *before* the sub-layer, which is more stable for deep stacks. The choice matters at scale but not for the picture.

Together, the residual and the LayerNorm form the **residual stream** &mdash; the conceptual through-line of the transformer. You can think of the residual stream as a long vector that lives at each token position throughout the entire stack of layers. Each sub-block reads from the residual stream, computes something based on it, and *adds* the result back into the residual stream. The stream is the medium through which information propagates upward through the layers. Information that needs to survive from layer one to layer ninety-six just sits in the residual stream and gets carried along; information that needs to be transformed gets read out, modified, and written back. This conceptual model, popularised by Anthropic's mechanistic-interpretability work since 2021, is one of the most useful ways to understand what a deep transformer is *doing*. It is hidden in plain sight in the 2017 architecture diagram.

We have the full block. We have attention plus feed-forward, residual plus LayerNorm. We have stacked them six deep. The next act is what happens when you put two of these stacks back-to-back &mdash; an *encoder* and a *decoder* &mdash; and ask them to translate a sentence.
