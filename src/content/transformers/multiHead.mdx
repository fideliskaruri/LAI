# One sentence, eight different ways of looking

Drag the slider on the left from head one to head eight. Each step swaps in a different attention pattern on the right. Some heads attend to the *previous* token; some to the *next*; some to the *first* token in the sentence; some have a strong diagonal &mdash; each token attending mostly to itself. These are four different things to look for in the same sentence. The picture is a hand-baked caricature of what real heads do; the principle is real.

The single-head attention from the previous act has a weakness: it produces *one* attention pattern per layer. One pattern means one notion of "which tokens matter to which." But when you read *the cat sat on the mat*, you are simultaneously tracking many relationships &mdash; the subject of the verb, the determiner of the noun, the agreement of the auxiliary, the antecedent of any pronouns &mdash; and asking a single attention pattern to express all of them at once is asking too much. The 2017 paper's fix is **multi-head attention**: run the attention mechanism *h* times in parallel, with different projection matrices for each, then concatenate the outputs and project them back down.

Concretely, instead of one set of matrices Wq, Wk, Wv, the multi-head version learns *h* sets, indexed by *head number*: Wq<sup>(1)</sup>, Wq<sup>(2)</sup>, ..., Wq<sup>(h)</sup>, and similarly for Wk and Wv. Each head produces its own *N*-by-*d<sub>k</sub>* output. The *h* outputs are concatenated along the feature dimension and passed through a final projection matrix Wo that mixes information across heads and projects back to *d*. The 2017 paper used *h* = 8 with *d<sub>k</sub>* = *d* / *h* = 64. The total parameter count is the same as a single big head with *d<sub>k</sub>* = *d*, because each head is *h* times smaller; what changes is that the model now has *h* independent attention patterns at each layer rather than one.

The bet is that different heads will specialise. This bet has paid off in surprising and well-documented ways. A paper called *What Does BERT Look At?* by Kevin Clark, Urvashi Khandelwal, Omer Levy, and Christopher Manning at Stanford in 2019 went through the heads of a trained BERT model and assigned each one a job. They found heads that did **previous-token attention** (every token attends to the one before it; useful for picking up bigram context), heads that did **next-token attention**, heads that attended to **the period** at the end of the sentence (using punctuation as a kind of summary node), heads that attended to **co-reference targets** (pronouns attending to their antecedents), heads that did **syntactic dependency tracking** (verbs attending to their direct objects). Other heads did not have obvious linguistic jobs at all and seemed to fire on combinations of features.

The right pane runs through four of these archetypes &mdash; *self-attending*, *previous*, *next*, *first* &mdash; for the slider's eight heads. The first four are the four archetypes; the last four are repeats. The patterns are not learned, just illustrative. The picture demonstrates the *kind* of variation real multi-head transformers exhibit: the same six tokens being read for many different relationships at once.

Why eight heads, specifically? In the 2017 paper, eight was a convenient choice for *d* = 512: eight heads of dimension 64 each. The number is not magic. Modern transformers use whatever fits: GPT-2 small has 12 heads at *d* = 768; GPT-3 has 96 heads at *d* = 12,288. The ratio *d / h* stays around 64 most of the time, which suggests that 64 is roughly the dimensionality each attention head needs to do its job. But this is empirical rather than theoretical &mdash; nobody really knows the right answer, only that 64 works, 32 works less well, and 128 doesn't help much.

A small unsettling note. Not every head does a useful job. Several papers since 2019 have shown that you can *prune* about half the heads in a trained transformer without measurably hurting performance &mdash; some heads, after training, end up doing essentially nothing. The same body of work has shown that you can *re-train* a model with half the heads from scratch and it will perform almost as well as the full one, suggesting that the redundancy of having many heads is doing something during training that the final architecture doesn't strictly need. Multi-head attention seems to be partly a *trainability* trick: redundant heads explore different attention patterns, the best ones survive, and the network as a whole learns more reliably than a single-head variant would. The picture on the screen shows one consequence; the literature shows another.

We have attention. We have heads. The next piece of the block is the part that does the *thinking* between attention layers &mdash; the feed-forward sub-block, and the architectural plumbing (residual connections, layer normalisation) that makes everything stackable.
