# Eight years, eleven models, one ancestor

The lineage tree on the left has the 2017 paper at the root. Every node below is a descendant. The thread card on the right is a forward-pointer to the next chapter. Both pictures are static. The right one is the one to read carefully.

In **2017** Vaswani and seven colleagues published the architecture. In **June 2018** at OpenAI, Alec Radford and Karthik Narasimhan, Tim Salimans, and Ilya Sutskever published *Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training* &mdash; the original **GPT** &mdash; which kept the decoder half of the transformer, trained it on a large corpus of unlabelled text using the simplest possible objective ("predict the next token"), and showed that the resulting model could be fine-tuned for many downstream tasks. The pretraining-and-fine-tune paradigm was born. In **October 2018**, Jacob Devlin, Ming-Wei Chang, Kenton Lee, and Kristina Toutanova at Google AI published **BERT** &mdash; *Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers* &mdash; which kept the *encoder* half of the transformer, trained it on a *masked-language-modelling* objective (predict a masked-out token from its left and right context), and beat the state of the art on eleven different natural-language-understanding benchmarks. By the end of 2018 the architecture had two large branches.

In **2019** the lineage grew faster. OpenAI's **GPT-2** scaled the GPT-1 architecture to 1.5 billion parameters and showed that a generative language model at that scale produced fluent paragraph-long outputs. Google's **T5** &mdash; *Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer* by Colin Raffel and collaborators &mdash; argued that *every* NLP task should be framed as text-in, text-out and trained on a single encoder-decoder transformer. (T5 reunified the two branches BERT and GPT had split.) The same year, BART at Facebook AI took a similar position.

In **2020** the architecture exhibited its first surprise. OpenAI's **GPT-3** scaled to 175 billion parameters and demonstrated that, at this size, the same transformer could perform tasks it was never trained on *just by reading examples in its prompt*. This phenomenon, *in-context learning*, was not predicted by anyone. The same year, Switch Transformers and mixture-of-experts variants from Google showed how to train very large transformers efficiently by routing each token to a small subset of the network's parameters.

In **2022** the architecture reshaped the public world. **PaLM** at Google scaled the dense decoder to 540 billion parameters. **Chinchilla** at DeepMind, by Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues, showed that *most large models had been over-parameterised and undertrained* and proposed new scaling laws. **Claude** at Anthropic and **GPT-3.5** at OpenAI brought human-feedback alignment to the architecture. On **November 30** OpenAI released **ChatGPT**, which put a transformer in front of a hundred million users within two months. The architecture was now a household object.

In **2023** **GPT-4**, **Llama** at Meta, **Gemini** at Google, **Claude 2** at Anthropic. In 2024, **Llama 3**, **Claude 3**, **GPT-4 Turbo**, **Gemini 1.5**. In 2025, **Claude 3.5 Sonnet**, **GPT-4o**, **Claude 4**, **GPT-5**. The architecture, now eight years old, is the substrate of every frontier system. None of these models is, structurally, the 2017 paper. They have all switched the positional encoding to RoPE; they have all dropped LayerNorm for RMSNorm; many use grouped-query attention; some use mixture-of-experts at the FFN; some use Mamba-style state-space layers as occasional substitutes for attention. But the *shape* &mdash; *token embed &rarr; positional info &rarr; alternating attention and MLP blocks with residual streams &rarr; token unembed* &mdash; that shape has not changed. The 2017 architecture is to modern AI what the 1986 Rumelhart-Hinton-Williams paper is to neural networks generally. It is the substrate everything else sits on.

The thread card on the right points forward. We now have a transformer. We know what tokens are, what embeddings are, what positions are, what attention is, what the feed-forward block does, and what the residual stream carries. We know how an encoder reads and how a decoder writes. *None of this, by itself, makes a language model.* A language model is what you get when you take this architecture, train it on a very large corpus of text with the simplest possible learning objective, and ask &mdash; *what happens?* The 2018 GPT paper, the 2019 GPT-2 paper, and the 2020 GPT-3 paper are answers to that question, each at a different scale. The next chapter is about pretraining, scaling laws, and the phenomenon those papers exposed: that when you train a transformer at sufficient scale on sufficient text, capabilities emerge that nobody put in.

The architecture is half the story. The data and the training process are the other half. We turn to those next.
