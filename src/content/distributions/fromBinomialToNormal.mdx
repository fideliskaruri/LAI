# When the bars sharpen onto a curve

Keep the coin fair, $p = 0.5$, and watch what happens as $n$ runs.

At $n = 4$ the histogram has five fat bars: probabilities $1/16, 4/16, 6/16, 4/16, 1/16$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ heads. At $n = 16$ the bars are narrower and there are more of them, and the centre — around $k = 8$ — has gotten much taller relative to the edges. By $n = 100$ the bars are so narrow they look like the silhouette of a continuous curve. By $n = 400$ — if your eyes are honest about it — you can barely tell where the bars end and the *vermilion bell on the canvas* begins.

That bell isn't a guess. It's a specific Gaussian: mean $\mu = np$ and variance $\sigma^2 = np(1-p)$. The same two numbers that controlled the binomial's centre and spread now control the centre and spread of its continuous limit.

This is the *de Moivre–Laplace theorem*, named for the two people who proved it. Abraham de Moivre, a French Huguenot who'd fled to London after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, was the first. In $1733$ he was computing binomial probabilities by hand — useful for the gambling problems that paid his bills — and he noticed that for large $n$ the answer was very nearly

$$
\binom{n}{k} \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \cdot n / 4}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(k - n/2)^2}{2 \cdot n/4}\right).
$$

That right-hand side — though de Moivre didn't yet call it that — *is* the normal density, evaluated at $k$, with $\mu = n/2$ and $\sigma^2 = n/4$. He had stumbled across the Gaussian seventy years before Gauss made it famous, and he published the proof in a pamphlet that almost no-one read. Pierre-Simon Laplace, in his $1812$ *Théorie analytique des probabilités*, generalised it: not just $p = 1/2$, not just coins, *any* $p \in (0, 1)$ and any $n$ large enough, the binomial approaches a Gaussian centred at $np$ with variance $np(1-p)$. Modern proof: Stirling's formula on the binomial coefficient. The algebra is unfriendly. The result is one of the most useful in applied mathematics.

What does it *mean*, though? The bars on the canvas are heights of probabilities at integer values of $k$. The bell is a continuous *density* $f(x)$ defined for every real $x$. They don't live in the same world. So how can one be the limit of the other?

Here's the honest version. The binomial PMF $P(K = k)$ is a probability — a unitless number between $0$ and $1$. The Gaussian PDF $f(x)$ is a density — it has units of *per unit $x$*. To compare them at the same scale, you treat each bar as a thin rectangle of width $1$ (one whole unit of $k$), so its area equals its height, equals the probability. The bell curve, by contrast, gives probabilities through *areas under itself*, so the area of any narrow vertical strip of width $1$ approximates the probability that a random sample from the continuous distribution lands in that strip. As $n \to \infty$, the strip-areas under the Gaussian match the bar-areas of the binomial, term for term, with vanishing error.

The discrete becomes continuous by *exchange of currency*. The binomial pays in probability-per-integer; the Gaussian pays in probability-per-unit-$x$; in the limit, the exchange rate is one.

There is a beautiful subtlety in this convergence that's worth flagging: the binomial-to-Gaussian fit is good in the *bulk* — the middle of the distribution — but breaks down in the *tails*. Out at $k = n$ or $k = 0$, where the binomial's actual probabilities are astronomically small, the Gaussian approximation can be off by orders of magnitude. The Gaussian's tails decay like $e^{-x^2}$; the binomial's decay differently, more sharply. For rare-event probabilities, you want a different limit — which is the Poisson, two acts from now.

But for ordinary "how many heads, give or take" questions, the Gaussian is essentially exact, and it lets you replace gnarly factorial arithmetic with a smooth function you can integrate, differentiate, parametrise, and stick in the middle of a likelihood. That's why the Gaussian wins, in practice. It's *workable*.
