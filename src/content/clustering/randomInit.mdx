# Three guesses on the plane

We need to start somewhere. So: we start somewhere.

On the left, three diamond-shaped marks have been dropped on the plane. These are our *centers* &mdash; our guesses at where the three clusters live. They are not in good places. They were placed by an indifferent random choice. In a real implementation you'd write `centroids = random.sample(points, k)` or something close to it; the spirit is the same. You guess, you don't worry about whether you guessed well, and you let the algorithm fix it for you.

Each point on the plane has been quietly coloured by *the nearest center*. If a point is closer to the vermilion center than to either of the others, it's vermilion. The faint background tint shows the same idea spatially: each region of the plane is shaded by which center is closest to that region. Mathematicians call these regions *Voronoi cells* (after Georgy Voronoi, who wrote about them in 1908). Geographers have used the same construction since the cholera maps of the 1850s &mdash; "which water pump is closest to this address" is exactly the same question.

Try it: drag any of the three centers.

The colours follow you in real time. Move the vermilion center across the plane and watch a column of points flip from vermilion to blue. The boundaries are *exactly* the perpendicular bisectors between pairs of centers &mdash; nothing fancier than middle-school geometry once you write it out.

So already, with no algorithm at all, just *be closest to a center*, we have a clustering. It's a *bad* clustering &mdash; the centers are in silly places, the cluster sizes are uneven, the colours don't line up with what your eye saw. But it is a clustering. The next two acts will turn it into a good one.
