# The local minimum trap

Press "Reset and try again" on the left a few times.

Each press starts the algorithm from a *different* set of initial centers and runs it to convergence. Most of the time, you'll get the obvious answer &mdash; three clusters that line up with what your eye saw at the beginning of the chapter. The inertia readout will be low. Everything looks settled.

But every so often you'll see something off.

Sometimes one of the natural clusters gets *split between two centers* &mdash; two centers crowd into the same group of points and divide it down the middle, leaving the third center alone with the remaining two real clusters. Sometimes two natural clusters get *merged* onto a single center. The colours don't match what your eye saw. The inertia is higher than it should be. The drift is zero, the algorithm is convinced it has converged, and the answer is wrong.

This is the *local minimum trap*, and it is K-means' single most-quoted weakness. The algorithm doesn't search for the *best* partition. It searches for *a* partition such that no single point is in the wrong cluster &mdash; a partition that's locally stable. Many partitions are locally stable. K-means picks one of them. Which one depends on where you started.

The standard fix is an initialization scheme called **K-means++**, published by David Arthur and Sergei Vassilvitskii in 2007. The idea: pick the first center randomly, then pick each subsequent center with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest already-chosen center. Centers spread out. The bad initializations become rare. The expected inertia is provably within a logarithmic factor of optimal. Most modern implementations of `KMeans` use ++ by default.

A second fix is simpler and almost as good: just run the algorithm many times from different random starts, and keep the one with the lowest final inertia. `scikit-learn` defaults to ten restarts.

And then there's the question we've been quietly ducking the entire chapter.

We picked `k = 3`. We knew there were three clusters because *we built the data and we can see them*. In real applications you don't get to know. Choosing `k` is its own subfield &mdash; the elbow method, the silhouette score, the gap statistic, BIC for the Gaussian-mixture version. None of them is decisive. The honest answer is usually: try a few, look at the results, talk to a domain expert. The math doesn't tell you how many clusters your data has. It tells you what happens if you commit to a number.
