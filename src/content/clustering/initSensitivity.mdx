# The local minimum trap

Press "Reset and try again" on the left a few times.

Each press starts the algorithm from a *different* set of initial centers and runs it to convergence. Most of the time, you'll get the obvious answer &mdash; three clusters that line up with what your eye saw at the beginning of the chapter. The inertia readout will be low. Everything looks settled.

But every so often you'll see something off.

Sometimes one of the natural clusters gets *split between two centers* &mdash; two centers crowd into the same group of points and divide it down the middle, leaving the third center alone with the remaining two real clusters. Sometimes two natural clusters get *merged* onto a single center. The colours don't match what your eye saw. The inertia is higher than it should be. The drift is zero, the algorithm is convinced it has converged, and the answer is wrong.

This is the *local minimum trap*, and it is K-means' single most-quoted weakness. The algorithm doesn't search for the *best* partition. It searches for *a* partition such that no single point is in the wrong cluster &mdash; a partition that's locally stable. Many partitions are locally stable. K-means picks one of them. Which one depends on where you started.

Most modern implementations spread the initial centers out — pick one at random, then pick the next far from it, and so on (this is the K-means++ refinement, 2007). They also run the algorithm several times from different random starts and keep the best. The "right number of clusters" is a deeper question we'll return to when we hit Gaussian mixtures.
