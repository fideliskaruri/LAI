# Where we left off

This is the picture from the end of the last chapter. Same parabola, $f(x) = x^2$. Same two draggable points. The chord between them, when they're apart, has whatever slope rise-over-run says it has. As you pull B toward A, the chord rotates. The closer B gets to A, the more the chord stops looking like something that *cuts across* the curve and starts looking like something that *grazes* it.

Pull B all the way to A. The two points merge. The chord becomes the tangent. The slope the canvas shows you is the slope of the curve *at A*.

That number — whatever the slope of the secant approaches as the second point slides into the first — is what we're going to spend the next several pages naming, defining, and using. It has a name from Newton (*fluxion*), a notation from Leibniz (`dy/dx`), and a third name from a French-speaking Italian named Joseph-Louis Lagrange working a hundred and twenty years later, who put a little tick after the function name and called it the *prime*. We'll mostly use Lagrange's. It's the cleanest of the three for typing.

But hold on. There's a real subtlety here, and it deserves the second of attention.

What we just did — *bring B to A and see what the chord's slope becomes* — looks innocent. It is not. If you bring B exactly to A, you have one point, no chord, and a rise-over-run of `0/0`, which is meaningless. The whole point of calculus is to be careful about this. We don't ever *actually* set B equal to A. We let B *approach* A and ask what the slope is *approaching*. That's the *limit*. And if the secant's slope happens to approach a single number — most of the time it does — that number is the slope of the tangent.

(For a beautifully behaved function like $x^2$, this is so docile that you barely notice the move. For weirder functions — corners, cusps, jumps — the limit doesn't exist and the derivative doesn't either. The function has to be *differentiable* at A for any of this to mean anything. The platform sticks to differentiable functions because that's where machine learning lives; the pathologies are real but they aren't the point.)

So: same move, same panel, same parabola. Pull B to A. Watch the chord become a tangent. The badge in the corner will tell you when they've merged.

A short admission: I keep wanting to say "and then they touch, exactly, and you have the answer." It's hard not to. The visual is so compelling — the points slide into each other; the chord rotates the last tiny amount and *is* the tangent. But that's not what we're going to write down. What we're going to write down is the *limit*, and the limit is a slightly more careful object that doesn't require the two points ever to actually merge. It just asks what the chord's slope is *trending toward*. The picture and the algebra match because, for the functions in this chapter, the trend has a single clean answer.

This was the move Newton wrote down in his 1666 notebooks and called *fluxion*. The move Leibniz wrote down in 1684 and called *the differential*. The move that took mathematicians another century and a half to put on a fully rigorous footing — Cauchy in the 1820s, Weierstrass in the 1860s. (The history of calculus is, in one frame, the story of the math being right for two hundred years before the foundations were nailed down. The instinct was correct. The bookkeeping took a while.)

Pull B to A. When the badge flips to CHORD → TANGENT, the panel is showing you the slope of the parabola at A. Read it. Notice it equals $2 \cdot x_A$. That's the pattern. We're about to name it.

Now we name what falls out.
