# The year he didn't publish

It's the summer of 1666 and Cambridge has been closed by an outbreak of plague. Isaac Newton, twenty-three years old, has been sent home — back to his mother's farm in Lincolnshire, a draughty stone house at Woolsthorpe Manor where he spent his childhood reading books instead of helping with the sheep.

What he does that year, and the year after, has come to be called *annus mirabilis* — the marvellous year. He works out a theory of colour by pulling a prism out of a fair stall and pointing it at a hole in his window shutter. He works out the inverse-square law of gravity. And he invents, more or less from scratch, the math of changing things. He calls it *the method of fluxions*.

He does not publish.

I want to sit with that for a second, because it gets glossed over. The young man, alone, who is unquestionably the most powerful mathematician in Europe, invents the calculus — and then puts his notebooks on a shelf and works on alchemy for the next two decades. He shows the work to friends. He uses it privately in *Principia* (1687), but written in geometric disguise so that he doesn't have to explain the move. He doesn't share the method.

(Newton was famously hostile to criticism. He'd been burned once by a public dispute about his colour theory and decided publishing was for the birds. It is one of the all-time strange decisions in the history of science, and the price he paid for it took the rest of his life.)

Meanwhile, somewhere on the continent, a young polymath named Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz has been thinking about the same thing. He's a diplomat by trade — a lawyer who keeps wandering into mathematics whenever Europe leaves him alone. Independently of Newton, working from completely different starting principles, he gets to the same place. Then he publishes. We'll meet him in the next act.

This chapter is the answer to the question Galileo couldn't answer. *How fast, right now?* Drag a couple of points together; watch a chord become a tangent; name the limit. That's it. That's calculus.

You already did the secant-to-tangent move once. We'll do it again, name what falls out, and from there work out a small handful of rules that let us crank derivatives mechanically — for any function we can write down — and end the chapter with a 2D picture that turns the derivative into a *vector* called the gradient. Which is the thing every machine-learning algorithm in the rest of the platform will be following downhill.

But first, the other discoverer.
