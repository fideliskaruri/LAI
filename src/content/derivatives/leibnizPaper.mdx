# The notation we still use

Eighteen years after Newton's *annus mirabilis*, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz publishes a six-page paper in a small Leipzig journal called *Acta Eruditorum*. October 1684. The title is a glorious Latin mouthful: *Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis, itemque tangentibus, quae nec fractas, nec irrationales quantitates moratur*. A new method for maxima, minima, and tangents, that doesn't get held up by fractions or irrationals.

It is the first appearance of the calculus in print.

And it is — this matters — *better* than Newton's version, on the only axis that ends up mattering: the notation.

Newton wrote his rates of change with a dot over the variable, the way a physicist sometimes still does. Leibniz wrote `dy/dx`. Read it out loud: "the change in y, over the change in x." It looks like a fraction because it is one, in spirit — it's the limit of an actual fraction, the rise over the run from the secant we just looked at. The notation *carries the geometric picture*. You can do algebra with it. You can multiply both sides by `dx`. You can chain it. The whole machinery of calculus, three and a half centuries later, is still mostly Leibniz's.

(I find this strange and a little unfair. The deeper insight was Newton's, almost certainly first. But Leibniz, who got there second, got the *notation* right, and notation is what gets taught. He even got the integral sign — that long-S, `∫` — which started life as a stylized "S" for *summa*. Every elongated S you see in a calculus textbook is Leibniz from a desk in Hannover in the early 1680s.)

The two of them did not realize, at first, that the other had also done this. Letters were exchanged, intermediaries were involved, the Royal Society got dragged in, and by the early 1700s it was an open war. Newton, by then president of the Royal Society, packed a 1712 investigating committee with his own supporters, who returned a predictably one-sided verdict. Leibniz died four years later in Hannover, more or less in disgrace at home and unpaid by the court that had employed him for forty years.

The historians have since adjudicated. Newton got there first; Leibniz got there independently; nobody plagiarised anybody. They both saw the move.

The reason we're spending three paragraphs on this is that you, reading, are about to use *both* notations all the time. Newton's prime — `f'(x)` — is compact; Leibniz's `dy/dx` is expressive. In a chain rule you'll want `dy/dx`. In a quick sentence about a parabola you'll want `f'`. Pick whichever fits the moment. They mean the same thing.

A few details about the 1684 paper itself, because the choice of venue is part of the story. *Acta Eruditorum* — "Acts of the Learned" — was a Leipzig journal Leibniz had helped found two years earlier, in 1682, partly because the existing journals were French and Dutch and he wanted a German-language scientific publication on the continent. *Nova methodus* ran six pages, in dense Latin, accompanied by no diagrams. It introduced `d`, `dx`, `dy`, the rules for products and quotients of differentials, and a worked example of finding a tangent. It was not, by any standard, easy reading. (The mathematician Jacob Bernoulli is on record as saying it took him two years to figure out what Leibniz had meant.)

What's striking is how *modern* the paper looks if you squint past the Latin. The notation is immediately recognisable. The product rule appears in essentially its current form. The chain rule is implied. The derivation is symbolic — Leibniz is doing algebra on these new objects called differentials, and the algebra goes through. Newton's contemporary work, by contrast, was buried in geometric proofs that are essentially impossible to read now without a translator. Leibniz's paper is a different *genre* of mathematics. It's the genre that won.

Now back to the move.
