# The slope of the loss

The picture on the right has changed shape. The bars are gone. In their place there's a smooth curve &mdash; a *parabola*. The horizontal axis is the line's slope &beta;<sub>1</sub>, running from somewhere slightly negative to about two. The vertical axis is SSE: the sum of squared errors that line would produce, if you used it on our twelve points. (The intercept &beta;<sub>0</sub> is being silently chosen behind the scenes to be the best it can be for each slope &mdash; otherwise the chart would be a surface in two dimensions instead of a curve in one, and I'd lose you.)

Grab the vermilion dot. It's a slider that lives on the parabola. As you drag it left and right, the slope of the fit line on the left changes to match &mdash; left pane and right pane co-move. Try to land the dot at the bottom of the bowl.

When you get there, three things happen at once. The line on the left settles into the best position the eye could find. The vermilion tangent line through the dot &mdash; the thing showing the *slope of the loss curve* &mdash; goes flat. And the readout in the corner switches to "MINIMUM" and reports the optimal &beta;<sub>1</sub>.

The point is the picture, not the readout. *The minimum of SSE is the place where the derivative of SSE with respect to* &beta;<sub>1</sub> *is zero*. That's why the tangent goes flat. That's the *whole computational story* of how to find the best line.

Writing it down. SSE is a function of two variables (&beta;<sub>0</sub>, &beta;<sub>1</sub>). The condition for a minimum &mdash; assuming the function is smooth and bowl-shaped, which it is &mdash; is that both partial derivatives vanish:

$$
\frac{\partial \, \text{SSE}}{\partial \beta_0} \;=\; 0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{\partial \, \text{SSE}}{\partial \beta_1} \;=\; 0
$$

Two equations, two unknowns. You learned how to do this in calculus class &mdash; differentiate, set to zero, solve. The fact that the equations are *linear* in (&beta;<sub>0</sub>, &beta;<sub>1</sub>) &mdash; which they are, because differentiating a quadratic gives you a linear expression &mdash; means we can solve them with linear algebra. They have a unique solution. We can write it down in closed form.

I want to be clear about what we're *not* doing here. We are not iteratively climbing down the loss surface, step by step, like a tiny ball rolling toward the bottom. That picture &mdash; *gradient descent* &mdash; is the picture that will run the rest of machine learning. But it's a picture we need when we *can't* write down the minimum in closed form. For linear regression, we don't need it; we have calculus and one piece of linear algebra and we're done.

Optimization &mdash; the next chapter &mdash; is what we do when this trick breaks. The loss isn't a parabola, or there are millions of parameters and inverting a millions-by-millions matrix is infeasible, or both. For now, we have the easy case. The parabola has a unique bottom, we find it by setting the derivative to zero, and that gives us our regression formula. The next act writes down what the formula actually is.

(One thing the chart hides: the parabola is the *projection* of a bowl-shaped surface in two dimensions onto one. The full picture is a bowl over the (&beta;<sub>0</sub>, &beta;<sub>1</sub>) plane. There's a single point at the bottom; that's the OLS estimate. The parabola you're sliding along is a slice through that bowl at the best-for-this-slope intercept. The full bowl, with two slopes' worth of degrees of freedom, is what we're solving for symbolically next.)
