# Numbers cascade

Drag the two sliders on the left. Each one is one of the network's inputs &mdash; *x*₁ on the top track, *x*₂ on the bottom. As you drag, every number downstream of those two inputs updates: the three hidden pre-activations *z*ₕ above the hidden neurons; the three hidden activations *h* below them; the output pre-activation *z*ₒ above the output neuron; the output *y* on the output side. Six numbers move when you move one slider, and the right pane writes out the arithmetic that connects them.

This is the *forward pass*. It's the thing the network does when you ask it to make a prediction. Pick an input. Feed it through. Read off the output. That's it. The "depth" of a deep network is just how many of these little weighted-sum-and-squash operations are chained in sequence between input and output. Two layers, as we have here, is the shallowest interesting case; modern language models can have a hundred or more.

Look at one hidden neuron, say *h*₁, while you drag. The pre-activation is

$$
z_{h,1} \;=\; W_{1,1}\,x_1 \,+\, W_{1,2}\,x_2 \,+\, b_1.
$$

It's a *linear* function of the inputs &mdash; a dot product plus a constant &mdash; exactly the same shape as the perceptron's pre-activation in the last chapter. The hidden neuron has, in effect, drawn its own *internal* decision boundary in input space, the line where *z*ₕ,₁ = 0. On one side of that line *z*ₕ,₁ is positive; on the other, negative. The sigmoid then squashes that signed distance into [0, 1]: positive *z* maps to near 1, negative *z* maps to near 0, and *z* near 0 maps to near ½. So *h*₁ is a *soft indicator* of which side of an internal half-plane the input lies on. The other two hidden neurons do the same with their own half-planes.

The output then takes a weighted sum of these three soft indicators, plus a bias, and squashes that sum. The output is asking the question: *given how strongly each hidden feature has fired, do those firings, weighted by V, add up to enough evidence for class 1?* It's the perceptron's question one layer up &mdash; but on a different feature space, one the network has *constructed* for itself.

Try a few specific inputs. Set both sliders to zero: the input is at the origin. The hidden activations will be near ½ for any hidden neuron whose bias *b* is near zero (since *Wx* = 0). Pull *x*₁ all the way positive: each hidden activation drifts to whatever extreme its weight on *x*₁ would predict &mdash; near 1 if *W*₁ is positive, near 0 if negative. Pull both inputs to opposing extremes &mdash; the kind of corner where XOR lives &mdash; and watch the three hidden activations split. *That's the hidden layer doing its job*: turning a single input point into a three-number representation, and the output is downstream.

The pre-activations are the perceptron-style linear part; the activations are the smooth nonlinearity. *Linear, smooth-nonlinear, linear, smooth-nonlinear* &mdash; that's the rhythm of every modern neural network. Two repeats here. Many hundreds of repeats in GPT-4. The pattern is identical.

The right pane spells out the same arithmetic in equations, with live numbers. It's worth comparing the two views side by side once or twice. The network diagram shows you *where* the signals travel; the equation panel shows you *what* arithmetic each neuron is doing. Both are the same forward pass, drawn two different ways.

Once you've got a feel for the prediction step, the next question is the obvious one: *how wrong is it?* That's the loss, and it's the topic of the next act.
