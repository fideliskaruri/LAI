# The problem nobody had labelled the answer to

Before we talk mechanism, let's look at the problem.

On the left, an English sentence: *the cat sat on the mat*. On the right, the French translation: *le chat s'est assis sur le tapis*. Hover any French word; the English word (or words) it corresponds to lights up on the left.

A few things to notice.

*Most of the alignments are one-to-one.* *cat* aligns to *chat*; *on* aligns to *sur*; *mat* aligns to *tapis*. Six French words pair off cleanly with six English words.

*Some are one-to-many.* The English *sat* is two French words: *s'est assis*. ("Has-itself sat," literally; French uses a reflexive past tense for "to sit down" that English doesn't have.) The French *s'est* and *assis* both look back at the English *sat*. The mapping is not always tidy.

*Some words duplicate.* English has *the* twice. So does French &mdash; *le*. But the first *le* attends to the first *the* and the second *le* attends to the second *the*, because of the noun each *the* attaches to. The model has to figure this out from context.

Here's the question this picture is supposed to land:

**Nobody labelled these alignments for the model.** In the dataset Bahdanau and Cho were training on &mdash; the WMT-14 English-French corpus, eight million translated sentence pairs &mdash; you had pairs of sentences. You did *not* have, "the word at position 3 in the English sentence corresponds to the words at positions 3 and 4 in the French sentence." That kind of annotation was rare and expensive. Older statistical machine translation systems &mdash; IBM Model 1 from 1990, and its successors &mdash; tried to *infer* alignments from sentence pairs alone using expectation-maximisation. They worked, sort of. They were brittle and hand-engineered.

Bahdanau wanted a model that *learned* these alignments as a side effect of being trained to translate well. No alignment labels. No EM. Just sentence pairs, gradient descent, and the right architecture.

What he and Cho did was insert, between the encoder and the decoder, a small differentiable mechanism that &mdash; at every step of producing an English word &mdash; computed a *weighted average* over all the encoder's hidden states. The weights were real numbers between 0 and 1; they summed to 1; they depended on the decoder's current state. *And they were learned end-to-end as part of training the translation loss.*

If the model produced a better next word when it weighted the French *chat* heavily while emitting English *cat*, then gradient descent rewarded that. After enough sentence pairs, the weights settled into the patterns you'd hope for. The picture on the right &mdash; with most weight on *chat* when emitting *cat* &mdash; emerged on its own from training.

It worked. The translation quality went up; long sentences stopped falling apart; and when Bahdanau visualised the learned weights as a heatmap, the picture looked like a real alignment table that a linguist might have drawn by hand. (We'll look at the heatmap in act 4.)

The mechanism Bahdanau introduced was called *additive attention*; the version that took over the field three years later, *scaled dot-product attention*, has slightly cleaner mechanics. Both have the same essential shape: at each output position, mix the input positions with learned weights. That mixing &mdash; the weighted sum &mdash; is the whole game.

The next act builds the dot-product version in detail.
