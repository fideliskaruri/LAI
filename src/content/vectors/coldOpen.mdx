# A quarrel in the pages of *Nature*

In the spring of 1893, two of Britain's most respected physicists were calling each other ridiculous in the pages of *Nature*.

Peter Tait — Edinburgh professor, co-author with Lord Kelvin of the canonical *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* — was furious. A young American chemist named Josiah Willard Gibbs had taken the elegant algebra of *quaternions*, which Tait had spent his career championing, and stripped it down into something Tait considered crude. A "monster," Tait called it. *Fit only for engineers.*

Gibbs, mild and bookish, mostly answered through colleagues. The fight ran for four years across the journal's letters page. R. S. Ball wrote an editorial in August 1893 trying to close it down. Nobody listened. The letters kept coming.

What were they actually arguing about? At the heart of it: how to write down a direction in space. How to add two pushes. How to describe what an arrow *is*.

We're going to spend the next hour figuring out what they were both circling. We'll start three hundred years before the fight — in 1586, with a Flemish engineer named Simon Stevin watching a chain of beads refuse to fall.
