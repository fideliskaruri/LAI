# One thread, four centuries

So here's the thread.

In 1586, Simon Stevin draped a chain of fourteen beads over a triangular wedge. He noticed the chain didn't slide. He realized that forces — pushes — combine the way arrows combine.

In 1637, Descartes wrote down a system of coordinates that let any point be described by a pair of numbers. The bridge from algebra to geometry was open.

In 1843, William Hamilton, walking to a meeting in Dublin, had a flash of intuition for how to multiply *triples* of numbers in a way that would let him do 3D rotation. He carved it into a bridge.

In 1881, Gibbs printed a pamphlet on vector analysis at Yale, stripping Hamilton's algebra down. In 1893 he and Tait fought it out in *Nature*. He won. We use Gibbs's notation today.

Now jump to today.

A modern language model — GPT, Claude, anything trained on text — represents each word as a *vector* with hundreds or thousands of components. The word `king` becomes a long column of numbers. Each number is one component, in a coordinate system the model invented for itself to capture meaning. *King* to *queen* is a direction. *Father* to *mother* is roughly the same direction. Add and scale these vectors and you get the geometry of meaning.

That's what's on the right of the figure.

Stevin's chain to GPT's `king` — same kind of object. Add. Scale. Measure length. Compute direction. The math hasn't changed in four hundred years.

The chapter is over. There are twenty-five more.
