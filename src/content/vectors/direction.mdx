# Pointing somewhere

Magnitude tells you how big. Direction tells you which way.

For a 2D vector, direction is a single angle — how far the arrow is rotated from the positive `x` axis, counterclockwise. The angle is usually called `θ` (theta), and you can read it off the components with a function called *arctan* (or `tan⁻¹` if you like).

`arctan` is the function that asks: *what angle has this slope?* If `θ` is the angle, then `tan(θ) = y / x`. To go the other way — given the slope, find the angle — you use `arctan(y / x)`. Your calculator has a button.

Drag the tip on the left. The arc sweeps. The angle updates.

This is why navigators have cared about vectors for a thousand years. A ship's velocity is its speed (magnitude) and its bearing (direction). A wind. A current. The whole science of navigation is bookkeeping for combining vectors — adding the wind to the ship's velocity, adding the current. Stevin's chain proved the math; Gibbs gave it the notation that fits on a chalkboard.

In 3D, direction needs *two* angles (latitude and longitude, more or less). In 1000D, direction is — well — a thousand numbers. We'll meet that.
