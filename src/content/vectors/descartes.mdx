# Two numbers will do it

Sometime in the 1630s, René Descartes was lying in bed — sick, the story goes, with one of the chronic illnesses that plagued him — when he noticed a fly on the ceiling. The story is folklore, fabricated by a children's book in 1998. There's no primary source.

What *is* true: in 1637 Descartes published an appendix to his *Discours de la méthode* called *La Géométrie*. And in it he showed something that nobody had quite said before. If you draw two perpendicular lines and call them x and y, you can describe the location of *any point* in the plane with exactly two numbers.

This sounds obvious now. It wasn't.

Until Descartes, geometry was Euclid's — figures, constructions, proofs about shapes. Algebra was al-Khwarizmi's — equations, manipulations, the math of unknowns. They were treated as separate kingdoms. *La Géométrie* showed that they were the same kingdom. A line is an equation. A curve is an equation. A point is an ordered pair of numbers.

The pair of numbers following your cursor is what Descartes invented. The plane is what we'll use to build everything else in this chapter.
