# The wreath of spheres

Stevin's argument is one of the most beautiful in the history of physics. He took a chain of fourteen beads, looped it over a triangular wedge, and let it hang.

No matter how he positioned the chain, it stayed still.

This wasn't obvious. The two sides of the wedge are different lengths — the left side, steeper; the right side, shallower. By all natural intuition, the chain should slide one way or the other. Heavier-looking side wins. But Stevin's chain didn't slide.

His argument: *suppose it did.* Suppose the chain moved one bead to the right. By symmetry of the closed loop, you'd be in exactly the same position you started — a chain with fourteen beads draped over the same wedge. So the chain would keep moving. *Perpetual motion.* Which is absurd.

Therefore: the chain doesn't move.

This is, in a hand, the first clear statement that the forces on a hanging chain add the way arrows add. Three centuries later, Peter Tait would call this elegant but insufficient. "Stevin was doing quaternions without knowing it," he wrote in the pages of *Nature* in 1893. Oliver Heaviside, on the other side of the argument, laughed in print.

We'll meet that fight at the end of this chapter.

Drag any bead. The chain redistributes. But it never *moves*.
