# The notation that won

Josiah Willard Gibbs was a chemist by training, a physicist by interest, a quiet man who taught at Yale and rarely traveled. In 1881 he had a small pamphlet privately printed — *Elements of Vector Analysis* — and circulated it among his students. He didn't publish it formally. He just wanted his graduate students to have a cleaner way to write the math of electromagnetism than what Tait was using.

Over in England, Oliver Heaviside — railway-telegraph engineer turned theoretician — was doing the same thing independently. By 1885 both had stripped Hamilton's quaternions down into what we now just call *vectors*. They had axes labeled `x, y, z`. They had a dot product. They had a cross product. They didn't have `i, j, k` anymore. The "quaternion sandwich" `q v q⁻¹` had become a matrix multiplication.

Tait was livid. He published essays in *Nature* calling vector analysis a hermaphrodite monstrosity. Heaviside, who never met an insult he wouldn't return, called Tait's quaternions a "hothouse flower" and offered to "freeze it."

Hover the rows on either side of the figure. Each row is the same physics, written two ways.

By 1900 the fight was over. Physicists were using Gibbs and Heaviside's notation. Tait's textbook is on a library shelf somewhere; you can find it. You haven't read it. Your physics class hasn't read it. Neither has anyone since about the First World War.

Quaternions did come back, eventually — in computer graphics, to handle 3D rotations more cleanly than rotation matrices. Hamilton would have approved.
