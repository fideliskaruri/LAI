# The two halves of the calculus are inverses

Here is the most surprising theorem in the subject. *Differentiation and integration are inverse operations.* If you take the slope of a function and then take the total accumulation of the slope, you get back the original function. And vice versa.

Stated cleanly, in the form Newton and Leibniz both arrived at independently — Newton by 1669, Leibniz by 1675, neither knowing what the other was doing — the *Fundamental Theorem of Calculus* says:

$$
\int_a^x f(t) \, dt = F(x) - F(a)
$$

where $F$ is *any* antiderivative of $f$: any function whose derivative is $f$. For our parabola, $f(t) = t^2$, an antiderivative is $F(t) = t^3 / 3$, because $\tfrac{d}{dt}(t^3/3) = t^2$. So

$$
\int_0^3 t^2 \, dt = F(3) - F(0) = \tfrac{27}{3} - 0 = 9
$$

Two lines. No Riemann sums. No limits. Just an antiderivative evaluated at the endpoints.

The canvas to the left makes the equality concrete. Top panel: the parabola, with the area-up-to-$x$ shaded vermilion. Drag the handle along the $x$-axis. Watch the shaded area grow. Bottom panel: the antiderivative $F(x) = x^3/3$, with a moving point at $(x, F(x))$, and a vermilion arrow showing the *rise* from $F(0) = 0$ to $F(x)$. *The shaded area on the top is numerically equal to the vermilion rise on the bottom, at every $x$*. That equality is the Fundamental Theorem, drawn.

Why is this true? Think about it this way. The area-up-to-$x$ is itself a function of $x$ — call it $A(x)$. As $x$ increases by a tiny amount $h$, the area gains a thin vertical strip of width $h$ and height roughly $f(x)$. So

$$
A(x + h) - A(x) \approx f(x) \cdot h
$$

Divide both sides by $h$ and take the limit:

$$
A'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{A(x+h) - A(x)}{h} = f(x)
$$

That's the *first half* of the Fundamental Theorem: *the area function's derivative is the original function*. The area function is an antiderivative of $f$. From this, the *second half* follows immediately: if $A(x) = F(x) - F(a)$ for some specific antiderivative $F$, then $\int_a^b f = A(b) = F(b) - F(a)$, which is what we wanted to show.

(I find this argument absolutely beautiful. It uses *nothing but* the geometric definition of the integral — area-up-to-$x$ — and the algebraic definition of the derivative — limit of difference quotients. The proof writes itself in three lines once you see that the new area added by nudging $x$ is approximately a rectangle of width $h$ and height $f(x)$. The "approximately" becomes exact in the limit. *That* is the engine of calculus.)

The theorem is "fundamental" because it unifies two questions that, on the surface, have nothing to do with each other. *What is the slope at this point?* and *What is the total under the curve?* are different questions, and yet they are linked by an exact algebraic relationship: each one undoes the other.

(Historically, the theorem is sometimes called the *Newton-Leibniz formula*. Both of them stated it independently. Newton's version, buried in his unpublished 1669 *De Analysi*, said that the area function's slope was the original function. Leibniz's version, in his 1675 notebooks, gave the formula $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$. Neither published it cleanly; both used it constantly. Bishop Berkeley, the philosopher, took both of them apart in 1734 for waving their hands at infinitesimals — "the ghosts of departed quantities" — but the result was so practically useful that nobody could ignore it. The rigorous formulation we use today is Cauchy's, from his 1823 lecture notes at the École Polytechnique.)

The practical consequence is enormous. *To compute an integral, you don't have to sum rectangles. You just have to find an antiderivative.* For polynomials, this is mechanical — the power rule for derivatives reverses cleanly:

$$
\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \qquad (n \neq -1)
$$

The $+ C$ is the *constant of integration* — antiderivatives are only determined up to an additive constant, because the derivative of a constant is zero. When you evaluate at the endpoints of a definite integral, the constant cancels, so it doesn't affect the numerical answer.

(For $n = -1$, $\int x^{-1} \, dx = \ln|x| + C$, the natural logarithm. The fact that the integral of $1/x$ is the log is one of the strangest accidents of calculus, and one of the reasons $e$ shows up everywhere. We'll meet that story in *Optimization*.)

The catch — and we will spend the entire next act on this catch — is that *not every function has a closed-form antiderivative*. Differentiation is mechanical: given any function written in elementary terms, you can mechanically compute its derivative. Integration is *not* — for many functions, no expression in elementary terms exists for the antiderivative, even though the antiderivative exists. The bell curve is the canonical example. We'll see it.

But for everything polynomial, everything trigonometric, everything exponential, and most reasonable combinations thereof — the Fundamental Theorem turns integration into a hunt for the antiderivative. And once you find it, the integral is just a subtraction.

Onward, to the catch.
