# The grid follows

Grab one of the basis vectors on the left and drag it. Watch what happens to the whole plane.

Every grid line bends to keep its relationship to the new î and ĵ. The transformed grid is what the original grid would have been, *if î and ĵ had always been there*.

Now look at the four numbers in the top-left readout. As you drag, they update — they're the coordinates of the new î (left column) and the new ĵ (right column). Two columns of two numbers each. That's a 2 by 2 matrix.

Here's the secret Cayley was sitting on: *that matrix is the transformation*. Not a description of it. Not a way of computing it. The matrix and the transformation are the same object, looked at from two different angles. The columns of the matrix are literally the new positions of î and ĵ.

So when someone hands you a 2 by 2 matrix — any four numbers in a grid — they're handing you a deformation of the plane. The first column tells you where î went; the second column tells you where ĵ went. That's it. There's nothing else.

Try dragging the two arrows on top of each other. Watch the grid collapse. The whole plane is being squashed onto a single line — which is to say, two basis vectors that point the same direction no longer span a plane, they only span a line. The matrix corresponding to that has a special property: its determinant is zero. We'll come back to that at the end.

For now, just play. Get a feel for which numbers in the matrix correspond to which parts of the deformation.
