# Scale, shear

Two more basic motions, each with its own slider.

The first slider — *k* — is uniform scale. When *k* is 2, î doubles in length and so does ĵ; the whole grid stretches by a factor of two. When *k* is 0.5, everything halves. When *k* is negative, the plane flips through the origin. When *k* is zero, the whole plane crushes to a single point at the origin. (Try it. The arrows vanish; the readout marks the collapse.) A uniform scale is the simplest transformation that doesn't keep areas — areas grow as `k²`.

The second slider — *s* — is *shear*. A shear is a strange motion: it leaves î completely alone, sitting where it was at (1, 0), but it tilts ĵ to the side. With *s* set to 0.5, ĵ goes from (0, 1) to (0.5, 1) — it slants to the right while staying the same vertical height. The grid gets *raked over* like wheat in a wind.

Slide *s* and watch what happens to the unit square. It turns into a parallelogram. Crucially, its base and its height haven't changed — and the area of a parallelogram is base times height, regardless of how slanted it is. So a pure shear *does* preserve areas, even though it bends every shape inside the plane.

(This is the move that runs the engineering of shear in materials science, the visual deformation of a font being italicised, and — eventually — the way a neural network deforms its input space layer by layer.)

Try both sliders at once. Scale by 1.5, shear by 0.3. The matrix updates. The grid shows the combined effect. The pattern that emerges: any 2 by 2 matrix can be built from these elementary motions — rotations, scales, shears — composed together. That's a real theorem — the SVD decomposition, M = UΣV^T as (rotation)(scale)(rotation), holds for any matrix. For now it's enough to recognize that *every* matrix is some combination of these moves.
