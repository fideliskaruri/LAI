# Show, don't tell

Step through the dataset card on the left. Each entry is a single (prompt, demonstration) pair, written *by a person*. Not generated by another model. Not scraped from the internet. Written, in actual sentences, by a contractor sitting at a computer somewhere in San Francisco or Nairobi or Manila in 2021 and 2022, paid by the hour to play the role of *the ideal assistant*. There are six examples here for you to look at. The real datasets at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have tens of thousands of pairs apiece.

On the right is a held-out test prompt &mdash; one the model never saw during training &mdash; with a toggle. *Before* the toggle, the base model launches into a meta-explanation of what bios are and how they tend to be constructed, the kind of answer a high-school essay might give. *After* the toggle, the same model writes the bio. One line, on task. The shift between *before* and *after* is what the dataset on the left bought.

The technique has a name. It's called **supervised fine-tuning**, or SFT, and it is the first of the three RLHF training stages. The name is more impressive than the operation it describes. SFT is just standard next-word-prediction training &mdash; the same objective the model was pretrained on &mdash; but the data is no longer "the entire internet." The data is now a small, carefully curated set of demonstrations of the behaviour we want. Pretraining teaches the model *what English sounds like and what facts there are*. SFT teaches the model *what a useful reply looks like*. Both phases use the same loss function. They just point the model at different distributions.

The history of how SFT became a thing is short and worth telling. In late 2019 and early 2020, **Daniel Ziegler**, **Nisan Stiennon**, and colleagues at OpenAI noticed that if you fine-tuned the small GPT-2 on a few thousand human-written examples of how to summarise a Reddit post, the resulting model summaries were dramatically better than the base GPT-2's. They wrote up the result, almost as an aside, in a paper that focused on what came after &mdash; reward modelling and PPO &mdash; but the SFT step kept growing in importance. By the InstructGPT paper in March 2022, OpenAI's contractor team had grown to about forty people writing roughly thirteen thousand demonstration pairs over six months. The contractor instructions ran to several pages: be helpful, be safe, be honest, be concise, be polite. Don't refuse questions that aren't actually dangerous. Don't moralise. Don't pretend you have personal experiences you can't have had. The contractors weren't writing answers to a fixed list of questions &mdash; they were trying to *show*, by example, what the assistant should do across the entire range of things people might ask.

The cost of this is worth being honest about. The dataset is small. The contractors are well-paid by the standards of crowdwork &mdash; tens of dollars an hour at the going rate &mdash; but the volume of writing is staggering, and writing for an assistant is not the same as writing for yourself. Contractors at scale companies have been interviewed about the work; a recurring theme is the strain of having to write *carefully*, in someone else's voice, all day, for a system that may or may not credit them. Anthropic's published acknowledgements thank their data team explicitly. The other labs are quieter about it, which is its own choice.

A second cost is *coverage*. The demonstrations cover what the contractors thought to write about, which is necessarily a smaller distribution than the full space of things users would actually ask. After SFT, the model is much more helpful on the kinds of questions that look like the training pairs (advice, summaries, code snippets, polite emails) and not much better on the kinds it didn't see (intricate domain-specific queries, multi-step planning, the long tail). This is the *out-of-coverage* problem &mdash; the model still drifts toward base-model behaviour when the prompt is far from what the contractors wrote.

So why use SFT at all if it's expensive and partial? Because *it's the right kindness to begin with*. SFT gets the model into the rough neighborhood of useful. It tightens the model's prior on *what kind of reply to give* (an answer, not a meta-essay), *what register to use* (a sympathetic helpful one, not a Wikipedia voice), and *how long* (proportional to the question). Without SFT, the next stages of RLHF don't work very well, because the model's natural distribution of replies is too far from anything a human would prefer and the reward signal isn't enough on its own to drag it across the gap.

Once SFT is done, the model is *much* better than the base model and *clearly worse* than the eventual product. It will still occasionally moralise, refuse harmless questions, write replies twice as long as they need to be. It will sometimes get things wrong in the contractor's voice rather than in the base model's voice, which is its own kind of failure. The next stage will fix this by giving the model something stronger than a list of demonstrations: *pairs of replies and a click*.

Drag the chip on the right back to *Before* one more time. Read the base model's answer again. Now drag it back to *After*. That's what one stage and a few thousand demonstrations buys you. It's the smallest useful unit of teaching, and it's the first move of RLHF.
