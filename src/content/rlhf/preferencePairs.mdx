# The kind of work a robot can't do

Click on a card on the left. Either one. There's a prompt at the top &mdash; *Describe the smell of rain* &mdash; and two completions below it. One is overwritten and a bit silly: *a bewildering panoply of olfactory phantasmagoria*. The other is plain and good: *wet stone, warm asphalt, a faint green note from the grass*. You pick one. The right pane ticks up by one comparison. The pair on the left changes. There's another prompt now, with two more completions. You pick again.

This is the *literal job* that filled the workdays of thousands of contractors employed by Scale AI, Surge, Remotasks, and the in-house data teams at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta from 2021 through 2024. Two replies, one click. Move to the next pair. The pace is brisk; an experienced labeller might do a hundred comparisons an hour. The work is harder than it looks. Some pairs are genuinely close. Some pairs involve topics the labeller doesn't know well, in which case the instructions say *defer to the one that sounds more cautious*. Some pairs involve topics the labeller knows extremely well, in which case the instructions say *pick the one that's right, even if it sounds boring*. The contractors are paid by the hour, vetted for English proficiency, given a few days of training on the rubric, and then turned loose on millions of comparisons.

The pile on the right grows. *32,000 comparisons, 127 contractors* &mdash; that's roughly the scale of the InstructGPT pile in 2022. Anthropic's first published Claude paper (April 2022) used a similar number. Later versions of every major lab's training pipeline use several million comparisons spread across many tasks and several rounds of model versions. The pile is the *currency* of RLHF; everything downstream depends on it.

A few things to notice about preferences. First, they don't require the labeller to write anything. They don't require the labeller to know what the *correct* answer is &mdash; only which of two attempts is *better*. This is a much weaker form of expertise than the SFT step required, which is why it scales. A person who couldn't write a polished one-line bio from scratch (most of us, honestly) can still reliably pick the better of two attempts. The signal you get from a comparison is weaker than from a demonstration &mdash; one bit of information rather than a few hundred &mdash; but it costs far less to produce, and you can collect a lot of them.

Second, comparisons are *robust* in a way that absolute scores are not. If you asked a labeller to give each reply a number from 1 to 10 &mdash; a *Likert rating* &mdash; you would find that different labellers calibrate differently. Some are stingy; some are generous. Two labellers might disagree about whether a reply is a 7 or an 8 even if they would unhesitatingly pick the same one between two options. The pair-comparison format sidesteps the calibration problem entirely. *Better* and *worse* are stable; *seven-out-of-ten* is not.

Third &mdash; and this is the deep one &mdash; comparisons let you build a *reward function out of nothing but human judgement*. You don't have to specify, in code, what makes a reply good. You don't have to write a rule that says "responses with fewer adjectives are better." You don't have to balance multiple objectives by hand. You just have to ask, *of these two, which?* The reward function is implicit in the pile of pairs. The next act builds the model that extracts it.

A practical note. The pairs of completions aren't generated at random. They're sampled from the SFT-trained model from the previous act (or, in later rounds, from earlier RLHF-trained models). The model is asked to produce, say, four or eight different completions to the same prompt, varying its sampling temperature, and the labeller is shown two of those completions side by side. So the comparison is "*which of two real things this model actually said*" rather than "*which of two ideal things*." This matters: it means the labels are *adversarial* to the current model in a useful way &mdash; they reveal which of the model's existing tendencies humans prefer and which they don't. You're not teaching the model what perfect looks like. You're teaching it which of its current habits to keep and which to drop.

The dataset that emerges from this stage is called a *preference dataset*. It is the input to the next stage. It is also, increasingly, *the* training-data product that gets sold and swapped between labs. There are companies whose entire business is collecting preference datasets to spec for AI labs. The going rate for a high-quality preference pair, as of 2024 reporting, sits somewhere between thirty cents and three dollars depending on the difficulty of the domain. Tens of millions of these comparisons get collected each year now. It is unglamorous work, done by people whose names will never appear on the published paper, and yet every single chatbot you've ever talked to learned its manners from a pile of files like the one you're growing right now.

Click a few more cards. The numbers move. The pile grows. We have the data; we need a way to use it.
