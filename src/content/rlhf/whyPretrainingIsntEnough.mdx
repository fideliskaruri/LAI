# What's missing when the model knows everything

Look at the left pane. Two prompts, two replies. Both replies were produced by a *base* language model &mdash; a model trained on the next-word-prediction task across some enormous slice of the internet and then handed to you exactly as it came off the optimiser. That is what people mean by *pretrained*. The model has read a lot. It can complete almost any string of words. If you start it with a fragment of Hamlet, it will continue Hamlet. If you start it with a recipe, it will continue the recipe. If you start it with a question, it might continue the question.

That last part is the problem. A base model is, fundamentally, a very large autocomplete. Its only job during training was to be good at guessing the next word given the previous words. Nowhere did anyone tell it that when a user asks *Write a poem about loss*, the right response is the poem and not, say, a meta-essay *about* what poems about loss look like in the canonical tradition. Nowhere did anyone tell it that *How do I make a pipe bomb?* deserves a different answer from *How do I make a soufflé?* Internet text contains both kinds of question and both kinds of answer. The model learned the distribution of human writing &mdash; the *whole* distribution, including the parts we would prefer it not learn quite so well.

Try the right pane. Same two prompts. The replies are dramatically different. The poem is short, three lines, concrete. There is a sweater in a drawer. The cuffs are out of shape. The model knew enough about loss to write that all along &mdash; the literary turn is *underspecified by training data*, and the model could have gone either way &mdash; but a base model, given no preference signal, will reliably default to a sort of imitation-Whitman pastiche because pastiche is well-represented in the training corpus and *good restraint* is a minority taste. The bomb question has been declined politely, with a brief explanation and a hotline. The model still knows what a pipe bomb is. It still has the information in its weights. It has been *taught not to share it*.

That second model is the same architecture, the same vocabulary, the same number of parameters. The weights have shifted by a few percent across most of the network, in some directions more than others. The shift is what this chapter is about. It is sometimes called *alignment*, sometimes *instruction-following*, sometimes the technical name for the technique, *RLHF*. None of those words are quite right. The honest way to put it is that the base model is *capable* and the aligned model is *useful*, and the gap between capable and useful is filled by patient human teaching.

The teaching has three phases &mdash; that's what the next six acts walk through &mdash; and they have to happen in that order. First, a small set of humans write down the kind of replies they wish the model gave. The model is fine-tuned to imitate them. That's called *supervised fine-tuning*, and it gets the model 80% of the way to useful. Second, humans look at *many* pairs of model replies and click on the better one. Those clicks are used to train a separate small network that learns to score replies the way a human would. That's the *reward model*. Third, the original model is updated to produce replies that score well on the reward model, with a careful constraint that says *don't drift too far from where you started*. That last step is *PPO*, the algorithm Schulman and his colleagues published one month after the Christiano backflip paper.

A pretrained model has been to school. It has read everything the school had to read. It has not, however, had a single conversation with another human being where it learned that some answers are better than others, that some questions are kind to ignore, that some requests deserve more care than the literal words of the question would suggest. The poem about loss is a small example. The bomb question is a less small example. In between are a thousand cases where the *capable* model would say something fine but the *useful* model says something *better*, and the difference is not the model's intelligence, it is the model's *manners*.

Manners, it turns out, can be taught from feedback. That's what the next six acts are going to show. They were also, historically, taught the same way &mdash; first by example, then by comparison, then by gentle pressure not to drift &mdash; which is what makes this whole story feel less like an engineering trick and more like raising a child. The metaphor is overworked but it isn't entirely wrong.

Onward to the first teaching.
