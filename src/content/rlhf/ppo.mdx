# A clipped step in the right direction

Press the **Step** button on the left. The vermilion dot lights up *Sample*, then *Score*, then *Advantage*, then *Clipped Step*, and then back to *Sample*. The whole loop is one *PPO iteration*. On the right, two curves track what happens to the language model as you do this thousands of times: its average reward (top curve, going up &mdash; the model is getting better at what the reward model wants) and its KL divergence from the base model (bottom curve, also going up &mdash; the model is drifting away from where it started).

The algorithm has a name and a paper. **Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms**, by **John Schulman**, **Filip Wolski**, **Prafulla Dhariwal**, **Alec Radford**, and **Oleg Klimov**, posted to arXiv as paper 1707.06347 on the twentieth of July, 2017 &mdash; thirty-eight days after the Christiano backflip paper. Same lab, same building, mostly the same circle of researchers. Schulman had spent the previous three years on a kind of detective story: how to make policy-gradient methods, the natural algorithm for reinforcement learning, *actually work in practice*. The trouble was that policy gradients had a long-standing instability: if you took too big a step, the policy would collapse and never recover; if you took too small a step, training took forever. Schulman's earlier paper, *Trust Region Policy Optimization* (TRPO, 2015), had solved the problem with a complicated second-order constraint involving the natural gradient and a conjugate-gradient solve. It worked, but it was painful to implement.

PPO was the simplification. The idea, in one sentence: *take the policy-gradient step you would have taken, but if the new policy is too different from the old one, clip the gradient.* The clipping is done on the *ratio* `r = π_new(a|s) / π_old(a|s)` &mdash; how much more (or less) likely the new policy is to take action `a` given state `s`. If this ratio drifts outside the range `[1 − ε, 1 + ε]`, the gradient is clipped to zero for that example. The typical `ε` is 0.2 &mdash; a 20% change per step is the maximum the algorithm allows. PPO is what you get when you say "*don't trust the gradient if it's asking you to change the policy by more than 20% in one shot*."

That trick was enough. PPO became the default reinforcement-learning algorithm at OpenAI for the next five years. It powered the Dota 2 system, the hide-and-seek environment, the simulated dexterous-manipulation work, and &mdash; the reason we're talking about it &mdash; every RLHF system at OpenAI through 2023.

Press **Step** a few more times. Watch what happens around each stage of the loop. The four stages are worth naming carefully because they're the architecture of the algorithm.

**Sample.** The current policy &mdash; the language model as it currently is &mdash; produces several completions to a batch of prompts drawn from a held-out set. These are just generated text. Each completion is a sequence of tokens with associated log-probabilities. You're collecting *experience*.

**Score.** Each (prompt, completion) pair is handed to the reward model from the previous act. It returns one scalar, the reward `r`. Long completions get one number for the whole thing; in some implementations the reward is also adjusted per-token by a learned value function, but the spirit is the same. *Each completion gets a score.*

**Advantage.** "How much better than expected was this completion?" The advantage is the reward minus a *baseline* &mdash; usually the value-function estimate of how good this prompt is on average. Positive advantage means the completion was better than average for this prompt; negative means worse. This is what tells the policy gradient *which direction* to push for each example.

**Clipped Step.** The policy gradient is computed against the advantage. The ratio of new-to-old probability is computed for each token. The gradient is *clipped* wherever the ratio is outside `[1 − ε, 1 + ε]`. The clipped gradient is applied to the policy. This is the only point in the loop where the model's weights actually change.

That's one iteration. The loop runs thousands of times. The vermilion dot on the right slides along both curves as you click.

Two things to notice. First, the **reward curve goes up** &mdash; the policy is, by the reward model's lights, getting better. That's the whole point. Second, the **KL divergence from the base model also goes up** &mdash; the policy is drifting away from where it started. The KL is a measure of how surprising the new policy is, relative to the old one. Zero KL means the policy is unchanged. High KL means the policy has moved a long way from its starting point.

This second curve is the price you pay for the first one. Every step of PPO trades a little bit of *similarity to the base model* for a little bit of *reward*. In moderation that trade is great &mdash; we *want* the model to move away from the base toward something more useful. But if the trade goes too far, the model exploits the reward model's blind spots and stops being a language model at all. The next act puts a leash on this drift, and shows you what happens when the leash is loose.

For now: press step. Watch the curves climb. Each click is one iteration of the loop OpenAI built to teach ChatGPT what to say.
