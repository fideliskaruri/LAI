# What came after — and why this chapter is already a little out of date

The picture on the left is three philosophies, in chronological order. They are the most influential descendants of the original RLHF pipeline, and any practitioner you might talk to in 2026 is using some mix of them.

**Helpful, Harmless, Honest** is the framing Anthropic published in April 2022 in their first paper, *Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback* (Bai, Jones, Ndousse et al., arXiv 2204.05862). The contribution wasn't a new algorithm &mdash; it was an *explicit decomposition of the goal*. Earlier RLHF papers spoke of "preference" as a single dimension. Anthropic argued you should label and optimise three things separately: is the reply *helpful* (does it answer the question), is it *harmless* (does it avoid causing harm), and is it *honest* (does it not assert things it doesn't know). The contractors scored each dimension; the reward model produced three scores; the training balanced them. The HHH frame is now the implicit goal of every major lab's safety training, even when they don't use those exact words.

**Constitutional AI**, also Anthropic, December 2022 (arXiv 2212.08073), made the move that really changed the economics. Instead of asking a contractor to compare two replies, you give a *short written constitution* &mdash; ten or twenty principles, like "*choose the response that is more honest*" or "*choose the response that better declines to help with anything illegal*" &mdash; and then you ask an *AI model* to compare two replies against the constitution. The model writes a critique, picks the better reply, and provides reasoning. Those AI-generated comparisons become the preference data. The Anthropic team called this approach *RLAIF* &mdash; Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback. It does not remove humans entirely (the constitution is written by humans, and humans audit the AI's critiques), but it removes them from the inner loop where the cost lives. Constitutional AI made it possible to train a system on the equivalent of millions of preference comparisons with a contractor budget that would have produced thousands.

**Direct Preference Optimisation** (DPO), Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell et al. at Stanford, May 2023 (arXiv 2305.18290), made the third philosophical move. It noticed that the entire RLHF pipeline &mdash; reward model, PPO, KL constraint &mdash; is mathematically equivalent to a single supervised learning loss on the preference pairs *directly*. There's no separate reward model. There's no PPO loop. There's no advantage estimation. You take the preference pairs and train the policy on a single loss function that turns out, after a few pages of derivation, to be implementing the same objective the long way around. DPO is simpler, faster, less prone to instability, and competitive with PPO on most benchmarks. By 2024 it was the most common training recipe for open-source models &mdash; Llama 3 used it, Mistral used it, all the smaller research labs use it &mdash; while OpenAI and Anthropic largely stuck with the PPO recipe (with some hybrid variants) because it's the one their infrastructure was built for and their best results were achieved with.

There are others, all worth a paragraph of their own and none of which I'll do justice to in one. **Rejection sampling** is the lazy cousin: just generate four responses, pick the one with the highest reward-model score, ignore the bottom three. Cheap, surprisingly effective, used as a warm start before full PPO at most labs. **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimisation), introduced in the DeepSeek-Math paper (Shao et al., February 2024), is the version that powers DeepSeek-R1 and almost every modern reasoning model: it replaces the value-function baseline with the *mean reward of the current batch*, which removes a fragile component of PPO and makes the algorithm dramatically simpler to train. **Online DPO** runs the DPO loss against fresh model-generated comparisons in a continuous loop, blurring the line between DPO and PPO. And the **alignment fine-tuning** terminology has, in 2026, broadened to include techniques far beyond RLHF &mdash; deliberation, debate, AI judges, automated red-teaming, all the things the field has invented to scale beyond the contractor model.

This chapter has been a tour of the *first* set of techniques in this lineage &mdash; the originals. None of them are obsolete; all of them are still in production somewhere. But it would be dishonest to suggest the field stopped moving in 2017. The Christiano backflip paper opened a frontier that other people have been homesteading on ever since, and the homesteading is still going. Whatever a 2026 model is, it was built on top of several of these stacks, with some institutional taste about which to use for which subproblem.

The next chapter changes the modality. RLHF, in all the variants we just discussed, is about what a *language* model says. The next chapter, **Diffusion**, is about how a model can generate not text but images, audio, video &mdash; how a model can be taught to *un-noise* a picture until what's left is a coherent generation of something that was never in the training data. The training algorithm is completely different. The geometric story is different. The historical anchors are different (Sohl-Dickstein 2015, Ho-Jain-Abbeel 2020, the cascade of Stable Diffusion in 2022). But the deepest lesson of this chapter &mdash; that capable models aren't useful until you teach them what humans actually want &mdash; transfers to the next one in a quiet way. Diffusion models, too, are aligned with RLHF-like techniques (the *Diffusion-DPO* paper, December 2023, showed how to do exactly that). One chapter changed what a model says. The next chapter changes what kind of thing it generates entirely. Both lessons sit on top of the same root: *people clicking on what they prefer*.
