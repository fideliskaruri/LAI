# The robot that learned to backflip from nine hundred votes

On the twelfth of June, 2017, a six-author paper appeared on the arXiv preprint server with a title that promised something both modest and strange: *Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences*. The lead author was a young researcher at OpenAI named **Paul Christiano**, joined by **Jan Leike** and **Tom Brown** from OpenAI, **Miljan Martic** and **Shane Legg** from DeepMind in London, and **Dario Amodei**, then OpenAI's research director. The paper was thirty pages, formal and quiet in tone. The headline experiment was a small simulated humanoid robot doing a single perfect backflip in mid-air, captured in a four-second video that was circulated, in some delight, on the corners of the internet that paid attention to this sort of thing.

What was remarkable about the backflip was not that a robot could do it &mdash; simulated robots had been doing tricks for a while. What was remarkable was *how the robot was taught to want to*. Backflips are notoriously hard to specify with a reward function. A naive engineer might try to write down rules &mdash; *get your feet off the ground*, *rotate three hundred and sixty degrees*, *land without falling*, *do it gracefully*. Each rule, taken alone, can be gamed. A robot that maximizes "rotation" learns to spin in place forever. A robot rewarded for "feet off the ground" learns to jump straight up. The space of clever ways to satisfy a written rule while completely missing its spirit is, in reinforcement learning, vast and exhausting and a little funny.

Christiano and his colleagues did something else. They removed the reward function. In its place they showed pairs of short video clips &mdash; four seconds each, side by side, of the simulated robot trying to do something &mdash; to a person sitting in front of a screen. The person clicked on whichever clip looked more like a backflip. That was it. The clicks were used to train a separate small network &mdash; the *reward model* &mdash; to predict which of two clips a person would prefer. Once that prediction worked well enough, the robot was trained against the reward model rather than against any hand-written rule. About **900 comparisons** were enough to teach the robot to backflip cleanly. "*Less than an hour of human time,*" the paper noted with some pride.

The technical idea has a name. It's called **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback**, often shortened to *RLHF* &mdash; the technique that would, a few years later, turn a fluent next-token predictor into something willing to follow instructions. It is the subject of this chapter. The shorthand is honest about what's happening: the model is being shaped by a *reward signal* (that's the *reinforcement learning* part) and the reward signal is being shaped by *people clicking on things* (that's the *human feedback* part). The full pipeline has a few more pieces &mdash; supervised fine-tuning at the start, a reward model in the middle, and at the end a way to take the *preference labels* (humans saying "A is better than B") and turn them into a gradient signal that actually improves the model. The OpenAI team called that last algorithm Proximal Policy Optimization, or *PPO* for short, and we'll meet it in detail in act five. But the kernel is what Christiano showed in 2017: when you can't write down a reward function, *let people compare two things and you'll have a reward function*.

The technique sat as a research curiosity for the next two years. Through 2018 and 2019 there was a quieter line of work at OpenAI that re-applied the same idea to *language models* &mdash; first to fine-tune a small version of GPT-2 to write convincingly, then to summarise documents in a way that human raters found genuinely useful. By September 2020, **Stiennon, Ouyang, Wu, Ziegler, Lowe, Voss, Radford, Amodei, and Christiano** published *Learning to summarize from human feedback*, in which a relatively small language model fine-tuned with RLHF wrote summaries that were preferred over the originals (the human-written reference summaries from the dataset) on Reddit-style posts. The team that wrote it was still small &mdash; nine people &mdash; and the technique was still niche.

Then came **InstructGPT**, in March of 2022. The paper, *Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback*, was led by **Long Ouyang** at OpenAI, with the same Christiano line of authors. The headline result was startling: a 1.3-billion-parameter language model trained with RLHF produced replies that human raters preferred over those of a 175-billion-parameter base model. Same training data underneath, same transformer architecture, two orders of magnitude fewer parameters &mdash; the smaller, *taught* model beat the larger, *untaught* one. The lesson of the paper was the lesson of the chapter: *capability and usefulness are not the same thing*. A larger model knows more. An aligned model is better company.

Eight months later, on November 30, 2022, OpenAI quietly published a free demo of a new chat interface called **ChatGPT**, built on top of InstructGPT's successor. Within sixty days it had a hundred million users &mdash; the fastest consumer-product adoption in history at the time. Five months after that, **Anthropic**, an AI safety company founded by some of the same researchers who had built RLHF at OpenAI, released **Claude**, which used a variant called *Constitutional AI* to do something similar with fewer human labels.

By 2024 every frontier language model that you could talk to was, underneath, an RLHF model. The technique that taught a simulated robot to backflip in 2017 became, six years later, the technique that taught every commercial chatbot how to be polite, useful, and refuse to help you build a bomb. It is one of the most influential ideas of the last decade, and it remains underexplained.

This chapter walks the pipeline. We will see *why* a pretrained model isn't enough on its own &mdash; what's missing when you talk to a raw next-word predictor. We will watch a contractor sit at a computer and click between two replies. We will train a small reward model on the resulting comparisons and see its agreement with the humans tighten. We will run the PPO algorithm that turns the reward model into a better policy. We will give it a leash, and see what happens when the leash is too loose. And we'll close with a quick look at three philosophies that came after: HHH from Anthropic, Constitutional AI, and the DPO trick that skips the reward model entirely.
