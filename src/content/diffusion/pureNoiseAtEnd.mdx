# The chain has two endpoints

The forward process has two ends, and they don't look alike. On the left end, at $t = 0$, you have your data &mdash; whatever the actual image is. On the right end, at $t = T$, you have *pure Gaussian noise*. That's the punchline of the previous act, but it's worth sitting with for a minute, because it's the load-bearing fact of everything that follows.

What does "pure Gaussian noise" mean? It means that the joint distribution of all the pixel values, marginal over what the original image was, is just $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Same distribution regardless of source. That's the standard normal on the right &mdash; the bell curve every pixel ends up obeying after enough noisings.

This is convenient in a way that bears emphasizing.

Suppose you wanted to *generate* new images that look like they came from your data distribution. You don't have a way to sample from that distribution directly &mdash; that's the whole problem of generative modeling. But you *do* know how to sample from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. It's three lines of NumPy. So if you had a way to *reverse the forward process* &mdash; to go from $t = T$ back to $t = 0$ &mdash; you'd have a sampler for the data. Start with noise, walk backwards, end up with an image.

The forward process is essentially a *bridge*. It connects two distributions: the data distribution (which you don't know how to sample from) and a Gaussian (which you trivially can). If you can learn the reverse step at each $t$, you can sample by starting in the easy place and walking to the hard one.

There's something almost ridiculous about this framing once you've seen it. The hard part of generative modeling &mdash; "how do I sample from a complicated unknown distribution?" &mdash; gets reduced to "how do I undo a known noise schedule?" The first problem is a research direction. The second one is a learning problem with a clear loss function and a clear target. That's the move.

It also has a nice physical interpretation, the one Sohl-Dickstein leaned into in the original paper. The forward process is like a drop of ink dissolving into a glass of water. The information in the spatial structure of the ink dissipates into the larger thermal bath of random water-molecule positions. Run it forward long enough and the system reaches *equilibrium* &mdash; a featureless, uniform distribution. The reverse process is asking: given the equilibrium state, can we recover the drop of ink we started with? Thermodynamically, the answer is *almost never*. Entropy doesn't run backwards. But you're allowed to *learn an approximation*, and the approximation can be good enough to look like a sample from the original distribution.

The right pane just draws the standard normal one last time, as a kind of formal goodbye to the data side. Up next, we start learning the reverse.
