# A compass that always points home

Here is the same idea told a different way.

Suppose you have data living on some shape in space &mdash; on the left, a ring. You don't know the analytic form of the density; all you have are samples. But you want to *generate* new samples that look like they came from the same distribution. One way to think about it: at every point in space, ask "which direction would I have to walk to make the probability density go up?" The answer is a vector &mdash; the gradient of log density, written $\nabla_x \log p(x)$. This vector is called the **score**.

On the right pane I've drawn the score field for our ring data. Every arrow points toward the ring. Arrows near the ring are short &mdash; you're already where the density is high, no need to move much. Arrows far from the ring are long &mdash; you're in low-density territory, walk briskly back. The score is a kind of compass that always points home.

If you had this compass, you could generate data by doing what's called **Langevin sampling**: start anywhere, take a small step in the direction of the score, add a little random noise, repeat. The noise prevents you from getting stuck at exactly one point; the score keeps pulling you back toward the data. Over many steps, your random walk concentrates on the ring &mdash; samples from $p(x)$, more or less, without ever having needed an analytic form for $p$.

The hard part is *learning* the score from data. You can't just compute $\nabla_x \log p(x)$ &mdash; you don't know $p$! This is where **score matching** comes in. The trick &mdash; due originally to Hyv&auml;rinen in 2005, but reborn for deep models by Song and Ermon in 2019 &mdash; is that you can train a network to predict the score *without ever evaluating the density*. You can fit a neural network $s_\theta(x)$ to be the score, using a loss that magically doesn't require knowing $p$. The clever variant Song and Ermon used, called *denoising score matching*, goes like this:

1. Pick a noise level $\sigma$.
2. Take a data sample $x_0$.
3. Make a noisy version $\tilde x = x_0 + \sigma \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
4. Train the network so that $s_\theta(\tilde x) \approx -\varepsilon / \sigma$.

Why $-\varepsilon / \sigma$? Because under the Gaussian noise model, the score of the noisy distribution turns out to equal exactly this quantity: the direction you'd have to go to undo the noise, scaled by how much noise there was. Predicting noise *is* predicting score.

Stop and notice what just happened. The denoising loss from act four &mdash; "predict the noise" &mdash; and the score-matching loss from this act &mdash; "predict the gradient of log density" &mdash; are *the same loss*. The function the network is computing is the same. The interpretation is different. From one angle you're undoing noise; from the other you're climbing a probability landscape. Ho et al. and Song & Ermon were, in 2020, writing papers about what turned out to be the same algorithm in two languages.

That kind of cross-derivation is, in my experience, almost always a sign that you're looking at something *real* &mdash; a fact about the structure of the world, rather than an accident of one particular framing. Diffusion models work because the data manifold has a *score*, and the score is something a neural network can learn from samples, and learning the score lets you sample new things from the manifold.

The forward chain we built in acts two and three was a way of *sweeping the score across all noise levels* &mdash; not just one $\sigma$, but every $\sigma$ on a schedule from 0 to enormous. The model learns the score at every level. At sampling time, you walk down the levels, using the score at each one to figure out which way to go.

On the canvas, you can think of the right pane &mdash; the field of arrows &mdash; as showing you what the model *outputs* at a particular noise level. The reverse sampling loop, which we'll run in the next act, is just: follow these arrows, downhill in noise space, until you reach $t = 0$.
