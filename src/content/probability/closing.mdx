# A thread forward

So here's where we are.

We started with a chevalier losing money in a Paris salon, wrote down the sample space of two dice, named events as subsets of that space, watched conditional probability shrink the denominator, noticed independence when the conditional didn't move, and let Bayes flip the direction of conditioning.

That's chapter seven. Eight more on probability and statistics to go — and the next one cracks open immediately.

Here's the question I want to plant in your head before you scroll away.

Knowing that the probability of rolling a seven is one in six tells you *how often* the seven happens. It doesn't tell you *how much it's worth* to bet on it. The chevalier's whole problem was that he was placing bets on certain outcomes and trying to predict whether he'd come out ahead in the long run. That's not a question about which outcomes happen. It's a question about the *average value* of all the outcomes, weighted by how often each one happens.

If I roll a fair die and you give me a dollar for each pip showing on top, what's a fair price for me to pay to play? Not one dollar, certainly. Not six. Somewhere in between. The answer is the *expected value* of the roll — the weighted average — and it turns out to be exactly $3.50$. Half of seven. The dead center of the bar chart on the right.

That's the next chapter. *Expectation*. We'll meet Christiaan Huygens, who wrote down the idea in 1657 — three years after Pascal and Fermat's letters — in a thirty-page Latin treatise on dice games. We'll meet Jakob Bernoulli, who proved that averaging enough rolls always converges to the expected value (the *law of large numbers*, 1713). And we'll figure out *variance*, which measures how much an outcome typically deviates from its average — how surprising a single roll is, on average.

The thread from this chapter to that one is the small bar chart in the figure. Each bar is an *outcome*; its height stands in for its *probability*; and the vermilion ruler at $3.5$ is the *expected value* — the average pip.

Probability tells us which worlds we're in. Expectation tells us what those worlds give us, on average. Together, they're enough to make most of the modeling decisions in machine learning.

See you in chapter eight.
