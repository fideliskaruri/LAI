# A nobleman writes to Pascal

The summer of 1654 was hot in Paris. In a salon somewhere off the rue Saint-Honoré, a self-styled "Chevalier" — his real name was Antoine Gombaud, and he wasn't actually a chevalier — sat across a card table from someone he was losing money to.

Gombaud was a gambler, a writer, and a man whose presence in the *salon* circuit depended on charm and a certain wit about numbers. He had a favorite dice bet: roll one die four times, and bet that at least one of the rolls will show a six. He'd been winning that bet for years. He understood it. He could feel the odds in his hand.

Then, at some point, he tried a variant. *Two dice, rolled twenty-four times. Bet on at least one double-six.* The variant seemed equivalent. Four rolls is to one chance in six as twenty-four rolls is to one chance in thirty-six. Same ratio. Same bet, scaled up. Right?

He started losing.

This bothered him for a while before he wrote to Blaise Pascal — a friend of friends, mathematician, son of a tax inspector, lately interested in mystical things but still sharp on numbers. Pascal wrote to his correspondent Pierre de Fermat down in Toulouse, a lawyer who did mathematics in the evenings. Their letters, that summer, are the first surviving conversation that treats *probability* as something you can do arithmetic on.

I should tell you up front: I'm going to draw the dice as a six-by-six grid of all the outcomes, and we'll count things. It's almost embarrassing how plain that picture is. Pascal didn't have it. He had words and ratios and a few clever tricks of counting. The grid is the picture you make once you already know what the answer should look like — once you've been told there are exactly thirty-six possibilities and they're all equally likely.

We're going to start there. And then we'll come back to the chevalier and see what he was actually getting wrong.
