# Which directions does a matrix leave alone?

Look at the picture on the left. Six little arrows fan out from the origin in different directions. Then a 2 by 2 matrix gets applied to each of them — the matrix is sitting in the corner, the one with twos on the diagonal and ones off it. The grey arrow is where each one started; the vermilion arrow is where it landed.

Most of them rotated. The arrow that started along the x-axis has tilted upward. The one that pointed mostly up and a little to the left has been bent rightward. That's the normal behavior. A matrix is a linear motion of the plane; it generally smears things into new directions.

Two of them didn't rotate.

The arrow along the direction (1, 1) — the line at forty-five degrees up and to the right — got *longer*. It stayed exactly on the same line, but stretched. You can see the dashed eigen-line running through it.

The arrow along the direction (1, −1) — the other forty-five-degree line, the one going down-right — also stayed on its line. Slightly stretched, but not nearly as much.

That's the whole question of this chapter: *which directions does a given matrix leave alone, in the sense of not rotating them?*

The directions get a name. They're called *eigenvectors* of the matrix. The amount by which they get stretched — the ratio of the after-length to the before-length, with a sign if the matrix flipped them — is called the *eigenvalue* for that direction. In symbols, when v is an eigenvector,

`Mv = λv`

The matrix doesn't bend v. It just multiplies it by some plain number λ.

Notice what the equation isn't doing. It isn't saying every vector behaves this way — almost no vector does. It's saying *some* vector does, for some particular λ. The whole game is to find the pairs (v, λ).

There's a question hiding in here that sounds like it should have an obvious answer and doesn't. Why would such directions exist at all? Why would a matrix that mostly rotates things have any direction it doesn't rotate?

For the symmetric matrices Cauchy was studying — and for the covariance matrices that come up everywhere in machine learning, and for many other tidy examples — the answer is: yes, always, and there are usually exactly *n* of them in *n* dimensions, and they're mutually perpendicular when the matrix is symmetric. For more general matrices the story gets more interesting; sometimes the eigenvectors collapse onto each other or move out into the complex numbers, and we'll wave at that later.

But the first question is the simplest. Can *you* find an eigenvector by hand?

Try it.

One more thing before you do. If you've seen this idea before — maybe in a linear algebra class, maybe in passing — there's a good chance it was introduced by writing down `det(M − λI) = 0` and solving a quadratic. I find that introduction backwards. The equation isn't the thing. The equation is *how you compute the thing on a computer*. The thing itself is the picture you're about to drag. There are directions a matrix leaves alone. The equation finds them. We'll get to the equation in a few acts, after the picture is solid.

(If you've never seen this before, even better. You're meeting it the way it actually felt to discover, which is to say, geometrically. Cauchy wasn't solving a quadratic in 1829 — he was trying to figure out whether the planets would crash. The quadratic came later as the bookkeeping of the geometric question.)
