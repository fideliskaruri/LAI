# Drag the arrow

On the left there's an arrow v, in vermilion, and its image Mv, in grey. The matrix M is the same one we just looked at — twos on the diagonal, ones off it.

Grab the vermilion tip and start dragging it around. Watch the grey arrow.

Two things will happen as you move v. First, the grey arrow obviously moves too — that's just *Mv* updating. Second, and this is the thing to watch for, the *angle* between v and Mv changes. When you happen to point v along the eigen-direction (1, 1), the grey arrow will line up with the vermilion one exactly, both pointing in the same direction. A halo will light up around the line. The readout will say λ = 3.

That's an eigenvector. There's nothing magical about how you got there — you just dragged your arrow into the position where the matrix's stretch and the arrow's direction happened to agree.

(If you can't find it by eye, hit Tab to focus the tip, then use the arrow keys. The eigen-direction is at forty-five degrees up and to the right. The display snaps when you get close.)

Try the other one too: (1, −1), the line at forty-five degrees going down-right. There the readout will show λ = 1. The matrix barely stretches that direction — by a factor of one, in fact, which means it leaves the arrow's length alone *and* its direction alone. That arrow is a fixed point of M, in the strong sense that Mv equals v exactly.

How is the screen deciding when you've "found" one? It's measuring whether the cross product of v and Mv is close to zero. In two dimensions, the cross product is just the signed area of the parallelogram with v and Mv as sides; when v and Mv are parallel, that parallelogram is squished flat and the area is zero. The threshold is around two degrees of slop, so you don't have to land perfectly.

What you're doing here by hand, a computer would do by solving an equation. In the next act we'll write that equation down. For now, just notice how it *feels*: every other direction wobbles, and there are these two little ridges where things lock in.

(There's something almost physical about it. When I first played with one of these I sat for ten minutes just running my arrow around, watching for the lock-in to happen. It's a feeling, not a calculation. Hold onto it. The algebra later will be friendlier if you remember it.)

A small thing worth pointing out, because it bothered me the first few times. The matrix M = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] looks like four numbers with no obvious structure. From inspection alone, you wouldn't guess that it has *any* directions it leaves alone, let alone that those directions are the ones at exactly forty-five degrees. The fact only becomes visible because you're sliding through every direction in the plane and watching for the one where the alignment locks in.

That's the deeper move that's worth holding onto. Eigenvectors aren't *part of* a matrix. They aren't sitting in the entries waiting to be read off. They're a structural fact *about* the matrix that you only see when you ask a particular question — *which direction does this matrix leave alone?* — and let the answer fall out of the picture.

Which means that for any matrix you'll meet later — a covariance matrix in PCA, a transition matrix in PageRank, a weight matrix in a neural network — the eigenvectors are a thing you go *look for*. They don't announce themselves. The whole intellectual move of "use the eigenbasis" is the realization that this hidden coordinate system exists, and that finding it is worth the effort because everything gets simpler in it.

We'll write down how a computer finds them — without dragging arrows around — in the next act. There's a piece of algebra Cauchy figured out that turns the geometric "which direction locks in" question into a polynomial equation.
