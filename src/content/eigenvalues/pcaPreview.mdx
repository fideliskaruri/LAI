# The cloud has a long axis

On the left, a cloud of two hundred and forty points. They're not scattered evenly — they're stretched along a tilted axis. You can see it by eye. There's a long direction, going from lower-left to upper-right, where most of the variation lives. And there's a short direction, perpendicular to it, where there's much less spread.

The two vermilion arrows are along those directions. They're not drawn from inspection. They're computed from the data, by taking the eigenvectors of a specific matrix called the *covariance matrix*. The longer arrow is the eigenvector with the larger eigenvalue. The shorter arrow is the eigenvector with the smaller eigenvalue. The eigenvalues themselves are the variances along those two directions — how much spread there is along each.

Press *Rotate into the eigenbasis*.

The whole cloud swings into a new orientation. Now the long direction lines up with the screen's horizontal axis; the short direction lines up with the vertical. We haven't lost any information about the cloud — every point's distance to every other point is preserved, because rotation is rigid. We've just re-described the same data in *its* preferred coordinate system instead of the one we started in.

That move — *find the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix and use them as your new axes* — is the entire content of Principal Component Analysis. PCA was invented by Karl Pearson in 1901, working on biometric data; he wrote a paper called *On Lines and Planes of Closest Fit to Systems of Points in Space*. Harold Hotelling rediscovered it independently in 1933 from the statistical side. It's been one of the workhorses of data analysis ever since.

The reason it's so useful is that the eigenvalues tell you *which axes matter*. Look at the readout — the λ₁ is much bigger than the λ₂. The horizontal axis in the rotated picture contains most of the cloud's variance. The vertical axis contains a little. If you had to summarize the cloud with one number per point — to compress it from two dimensions to one — you'd throw away the vertical coordinate and keep the horizontal one. You'd lose a small fraction of the spread and capture almost all of it.

That's dimensionality reduction. That's what every "embedding visualization" you've seen — t-SNE plots, UMAPs, PCAs of word vectors — is doing under the hood, in spirit. Picking the directions where the data lives and throwing away the directions where it doesn't.

We'll come back to all of this in the PCA chapter, which is up next. The math will get a bit more careful — what exactly does "variance" mean as a matrix? How do you handle the case where you want more than two principal components? What if the data isn't centered? What about non-linear structure that no rotation can flatten?

But the *insight* you should leave this chapter with is the one this picture makes plain. Data has a shape. The shape has natural axes. The natural axes are eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. The amount of spread along each axis is the corresponding eigenvalue. The whole geometric content of a dataset, projected to two dimensions, can be summarized by four numbers: two unit vectors and two scalars.

That's a lot of mileage from one piece of nineteenth-century algebra.
